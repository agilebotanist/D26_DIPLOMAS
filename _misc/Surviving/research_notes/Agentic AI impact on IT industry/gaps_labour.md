# Labour market gaps: follow-up pass with the browser scraper (as of 2026-11-17)

Reading guide. This file closes gaps listed in `labour_market_skills.md` and repeats nothing sourced there. Method: discovery through a public news feed search (headline, outlet and date only), then full-page scrapes with the browser scraper. Tags on every finding:

- [P] primary page read in full text in this session (company blog, press release, earnings-call transcript, the original interview or report page).
- [S] secondary article read in full text in this session.
- [SR] seen only as a headline with outlet and date on a search results page; the article body was not read (paywall, block or not attempted).

Who read what: I read the Microsoft and Copilot pages (section 1), the WEF press release, the Axios Amodei piece and the Lovable June 2026 piece myself. The pages behind sections 2 to 5 were read by delegated reader models that returned verbatim quotes; I used only items that came with a quote carrying the number, and I list their arithmetic and consistency warnings. Those items carry the same tags, with "(reader)" added. Nothing below comes from memory; recalled but unverified items are under Gaps. "Interest" notes who published and what they gain. Paywalled originals (Reuters, Bloomberg, WSJ, The Information, NYT) were not opened; where a finding rests on them it is marked as reported through another outlet.

## 1. Microsoft 2026: workforce actions and Copilot's commercial traction

### Takeaway
Confirmed for 2026: a first-ever voluntary retirement programme in spring (about 8,750 eligible, about 30% accepted), 4,800 layoffs in July, several hundred more in September, about 50 Copilot Studio staff in Israel called to hearings and the Nairobi engineering centre wound down in October, and on 26 October a Voluntary Exit Offer to about 60,000 eligible employees (about 27% of 223,000), for which no departure figure exists yet because the decision deadline is early December. Microsoft itself says the July roles were "not being directly replaced by AI" and frames the moves as cost control during a record AI capital-expenditure build-out; paid Microsoft 365 Copilot seats went from 15 million (January) to over 30 million (July), about 7% of the commercial base, and the company stopped reporting the seat count in October.

### Inferences
- Corrections to the leads in the earlier notes: "8,750" is the number eligible for the spring retirement programme, not a hiring-freeze figure; the 60,000 is eligibility; the only measured voluntary departure so far is about 30% of 8,750.
- Involuntary cuts confirmed for 2026 add up to roughly 6,000 to 6,500 (4,800 in July, 450 to 600 in September, about 850 at LinkedIn, plus Nairobi and Israel, whose numbers the company has not confirmed), against more than 15,000 in 2025. The 2026 story is a managed shrink with voluntary instruments, not a larger layoff wave.
- Microsoft does not claim that AI replaced these workers; the consistent explanation in company statements and in CNBC, GeekWire and Fortune is the capital-expenditure burden plus a shift of roles toward customer-embedded engineering. This matches the "budget channel" reading in the earlier notes.
- The teams that build agents are not protected: Copilot Studio staff in Israel and Copilot teams in the exit offer are affected while paid adoption sits at about 7% of the base. For the talk this is a clean example that building AI does not shield a team from cost pressure.
- The switch from seat counts to usage metrics in October coincides with the pricing change to seats plus consumption, so it has an innocent explanation, but it also removes the one comparable number outsiders had.

## 2. Historical analogies with data: radiologists, spreadsheets, compilers, and the economists' rule

### Takeaway
The radiologist case is well documented and cuts cleanly against a confident replacement forecast: nine years after Hinton's 2016 remark, residency positions are at a record, pay is up by about half and Hinton has conceded the timing, although the "shortage" itself is contested. The spreadsheet case shows substitution inside a profession (clerks down, accountants and analysts up), and Bessen's and Autor's work gives the rule: the sign of the employment effect depends on how elastic demand is, and since 1980 automation effects have outweighed augmentation.

### Cited Findings

Radiologists:

- The prediction: at a machine-learning conference in Toronto in 2016 Hinton said "If you work as a radiologist, you're like the coyote that's already over the edge of the cliff but hasn't yet looked down" and "It's just completely obvious that within five years deep learning is going to do better than radiologists... It might be 10 years, but we've got plenty of radiologists already"; also "people should stop training radiologists now". [S] (reader) [Fortune, published 4 May 2026, re-run July 2026](https://fortune.com/article/ai-godfather-radiologists-obsolete-salaries-up-to-571k-demand-growing/); [S] (reader) [The New Republic, Oct 2024](https://newrepublic.com/article/187203/ai-radiology-geoffrey-hinton-nobel-prediction) (written by a radiology resident, an interested party); [S] (reader) [Works in Progress](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/the-algorithm-will-see-you-now/) (essay magazine; the scraped page shows no date).
- The concession: in a New York Times feature of 14 May 2025 Hinton said he "spoke too broadly in 2016", meant image analysis only, and was "wrong on the timing but not the direction"; he now expects a "combination of A.I. and a radiologist" that will "make radiologists a whole lot more efficient". Reported second-hand; the NYT piece was not read. [S] (reader) [AuntMinnie Europe](https://www.auntminnieeurope.com/imaging-informatics/advanced-visualization/news/15746091/hinton-acknowledges-mistake-in-predicting-ai-replacement-of-radiologists) (radiology trade press); [S] (reader) [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/geoffrey-hintons-wildly-overconfident-ai-prediction-failed-now-its-a-lesson-in-humility/).
- Numbers since 2016: Mayo Clinic radiology staff "from around 260 in 2016 to over 400 today", +55%, while using more than 250 AI models (NYT via The Decoder). US diagnostic radiology residencies offered a record 1,208 positions in 2025, +4% on 2024 (NRMP data via Works in Progress). Active US radiologists up "about 10%" over ten years (Christoph Herpfer, UVA Darden, quoted in Fortune without a data source). Pay: average USD 520,000 in 2025, "over 48 percent higher than the average salary in 2015" (Medscape via Works in Progress); USD 571,000 "as of 2025, up 9% year over year" (Medscape's 2026 report via Fortune). The two pay figures come from different Medscape editions and are not reconciled. Radiology caseloads rose 25% from 2018 to early 2025 (Journal of the American College of Radiology via Fortune). [S] (reader) [Works in Progress](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/the-algorithm-will-see-you-now/); [S] (reader) [Fortune](https://fortune.com/article/ai-godfather-radiologists-obsolete-salaries-up-to-571k-demand-growing/).
- AI is present, not absent: over 700 FDA-cleared radiology models, more than three quarters of all medical AI devices; about 48% of radiologists use AI at all (2024 estimate); only 19% of those who piloted or deployed AI report "high" success. [S] (reader) [Works in Progress](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/the-algorithm-will-see-you-now/).
- Why the forecast failed, as the sources give it: benchmark accuracy drops by up to 20 points on other hospitals' data and 38% of 2024 models were tested at one hospital; most tools detect one finding in one image type; payment and liability rules require a physician's final read; image interpretation was only 36% of radiologists' time in a 2012 study of three hospitals; cheaper and faster imaging raised demand (imaging per 1,000 insured patients +60% in 2000 to 2008). [S] (reader) [Works in Progress](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/the-algorithm-will-see-you-now/).
- Counter-evidence on the "shortage": an analysis of 20,775 radiologist job postings (9 March to 26 May 2026) by RadBoard.io (a job board, so interested) found about 47% were re-posts, leaving 10,979 unique postings of which 1,470 stayed open over 60 days; "The shortage isn't national -- it's concentrated in places radiologists won't move to". Fortune cites the same source for 7,469 active postings as of May. [S] (reader) [AuntMinnie, Jun 2026](https://www.auntminnie.com/practice-management/administration/article/15827084/radiology-job-market-report-casts-doubt-on-us-radiologist-shortage).
- The analogy is in active use by vendors: [SR] CNBC, 4 Dec 2025: "Jensen Huang says an incorrect nine-year-old prediction about AI shows why it won't destroy jobs"; and contested by the profession when used the other way: [SR] Radiology Business, 9 Mar 2026: "Radiologists criticize Anthropic CEO's recent comments about the specialty".

Economists' summaries:

- Autor, Chin, Salomons and Seegmiller, "New Frontiers: The Origins and Content of New Work, 1940-2018" (Quarterly Journal of Economics): "about 60 percent of jobs in the U.S. represent new types of work, which have been created since 1940"; "the negative effects of automation on employment were more than twice as great in the 1980-2018 period as in the 1940-1980 period", while augmentation's positive effect rose only modestly; Autor: "There does appear to be a faster rate of automation, and a slower rate of augmentation, in the last four decades". [S] (reader) [MIT News, 1 Apr 2024](https://news.mit.edu/2024/does-technology-help-or-hurt-employment-0401) (university press office).
- Bessen, "Automation and jobs: when technology boosts employment" (Economic Policy, 2019): in cotton textiles and primary steel, employment "grew rapidly alongside rapid automation for a century or more" and then fell, an inverted U; demand elasticity falls from above 1 to below 1 as markets saturate; "While the rate of productivity growth influences the pace of change, the elasticity of demand determines the sign of the change". The column's figures are images, so no employment counts were captured. [P] (reader) [VoxEU / CEPR column](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/automation-and-jobs-when-technology-boosts-employment).
- Autor, "Why Are There Still So Many Jobs?" (Journal of Economic Perspectives 29(3), 2015, pp. 3-30): automation substitutes for some tasks and complements others, raising demand for labour where workers supply the complementary tasks; abstract only. [P] (reader) [AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.29.3.3).
- Carnegie Endowment overview (April 2026): repeats the 60% figure, quotes Autor that "demand for healthcare, education and computer code appears almost limitless" (NBER working paper 32140), and cites a Finnish study of firms adopting automation in 1994 to 2018 with employment up 23%. [S] (reader) [Carnegie Endowment](https://carnegieendowment.org/research/2026/04/the-ai-labor-debate-three-views-on-the-future-of-work) (think tank).

### Inferences
- A slide rule that follows from Bessen: automation raises employment in an occupation while demand for its output is elastic and unsaturated, and lowers it once demand saturates. Radiology (more scans), accounting after spreadsheets (more analysis) and the first decades of ATMs fit the first phase; bookkeeping clerks and late-stage tellers fit the second. The open question for software is where its demand curve sits.
- Radiology also shows the gap between task performance and job replacement: reading images was about a third of the job, and regulation and liability kept a human in the loop. The analogous items for developers are specification, verification and accountability.
- The radiologist story should not be told as "there is a shortage, so AI changed nothing": the shortage data are disputed and almost half of radiologists already use AI.
- Autor's 1980 to 2018 result is the counterweight to the comforting stories: over the last four decades automation's negative effect has been more than twice as large as before, so "it always worked out" is not what the data say.

### Gaps
- No count of programmers before and after compilers and high-level languages was found; no source with census or BLS series for programmers in the 1950s to 1980s. The compiler analogy currently rests on one opinion piece without numbers.
- The underlying BLS series behind Planet Money's 400,000 and 600,000 was not seen; the episode transcript was not retrieved.
- Bessen's actual employment counts for textiles, steel and automobiles are in figures that the scraper did not capture; the Economic Policy paper was not downloaded.
- No national series for the number of US radiologists by year (AAMC or ACR) was retrieved; the "+10% in ten years" figure is one economist's statement.
- The New York Times piece of 14 May 2025 with Hinton's concession is paywalled and was read only through trade-press summaries. One source says Hinton later predicted parity "in another 10 or 15 years" (via an Eric Topol post, not checked).

## 5. IT services headcount and fresher hiring, 2024-2026

### Takeaway

### Inferences
- The services firms describe the opposite of the "juniors out" pattern: TCS removed mid-level and senior staff and kept fresher intake at about 40,000 a year; Cognizant speaks of a "broader pyramid". In labour-arbitrage businesses AI is being used to push work down to cheaper, younger staff, at least in stated strategy.
- Net numbers hide churn: TCS's -23,460 in one fiscal year coexisted with 44,000 freshers onboarded, so gross exits (attrition near 13% plus the restructuring) were far larger than the net change.
- Fiscal 2026 was the trough for the Indian majors and the following two quarters show a rebound, so a chart ending in March 2026 would mislead. Wipro is the outlier.
- Accenture is the clearest data point for the Jevons side inside IT services: more AI work, headcount up 5%, and an analyst openly surprised that agentic delivery had not reduced it.

### Gaps
- Accenture's absolute headcount for August 2025 and August 2026 was not captured (only "+5%"); the annual report was not read. The scale of the 2025 "exits" (11,000 in headlines) is unverified.
- Cognizant's fresher target conflicts between sources (20,000 versus 25,000); the February articles' bodies were not readable.
- Wipro's quarterly figures do not reconcile across sources (242,156 versus 243,044), and the cause of the September 2026 drop is not given.
- 2024 calendar-year baselines were not collected in this pass; the series below start in mid-2025.
- All Indian figures come from press reports of company fact sheets, not from the fact sheets themselves.

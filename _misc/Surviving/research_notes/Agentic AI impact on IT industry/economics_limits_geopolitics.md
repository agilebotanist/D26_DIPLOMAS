## 1. Token economics: price per token versus spend per task

### Takeaway
The price of a fixed capability level keeps collapsing (Epoch: 9x to 900x per year depending on the threshold; a16z: about 10x per year), but the price of the newest top tier does not fall, and agentic use multiplies token volume, so bills per developer and per task rose in 2025-2026. The clearest evidence is behavioural: Cursor (2025), Anthropic (2025-2026) and GitHub Copilot (June 2026) all moved from flat plans to metered or capped plans and all met user backlash.

### Cited Findings

Price for a fixed capability (falling fast)
- [P] Epoch AI, data insight of 2025-03-12: the price to reach a given benchmark score falls between 9x and 900x per year depending on the threshold; GPT-4-level performance on PhD-level science questions (GPQA Diamond) fell 40x per year. Epoch itself cautions that the fastest declines happened in the last year and "it's less clear that those will persist". [Epoch AI](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/ai-inference-price-trends). Publisher: non-profit research institute, funded largely by Open Philanthropy-type donors; no product to sell, but an interest in AI-progress measurement.
- [P] Epoch data points (USD per 1M tokens, blended): GPT-3-level on MMLU: $60.00 (2021-11) → $20.00 (2022-09) → $2.00 (2023-03, GPT-3.5 Turbo) → $0.10 (2024-07, Llama 3.1 8B) → $0.07 (2024-10, Gemini 1.5 Flash 8B). GPT-4-level on MMLU: $37.50 (2023-03, GPT-4-0314) → $0.18 (2025-02, Gemini 2.0 Flash). GPT-4-level on GPQA Diamond: $37.50 (2023-03) → $0.12 (2024-12, Phi 4). The author calls the method "far from perfect" and expects the rate may slow. [a16z](https://a16z.com/llmflation-llm-inference-cost/). Publisher: venture investor with large AI holdings; interest in the "AI gets cheap, build on it" narrative.
- [SR] Stanford AI Index (using Epoch-linked data): a GPT-3.5-level query fell from about $20 to $0.07 per 1M tokens, more than 280x in about 18 months. ### Inferences
- Hypothesis (a) is half right. Tokens for a given quality level are not getting more expensive, they are getting dramatically cheaper. What is getting more expensive is frontier agentic work: newest-tier models hold or raise list price, agents consume many times more tokens, and vendors have withdrawn flat-rate plans that were effectively subsidised. The honest slide title is "cheap tokens, expensive tasks".
- The three repricing episodes (Cursor, Claude Code, Copilot) are independent confirmations that flat-rate agentic coding was sold below cost to heavy users.
- A practical consequence for teams: route by task (cheap or open-weight model for bulk, frontier for the hard 10%), because the price spread between tiers is now about 80x on Anthropic's own list (Haiku 5.5 $0.50 versus Fable 5.2 $40 output).

- Whether Claude Max / ChatGPT Pro style $200 plans are loss-making per subscriber: no reliable source found.

## 2. Capex and financing: who pays, and is it a bubble

### Takeaway
The four largest hyperscalers are on course for roughly $700B of capex in 2026, with all guiding higher for 2027; OpenAI and Anthropic both filed for IPOs in mid-2026 at valuations around or above $1 trillion while still loss-making at the operating level in 2025. Warnings about concentration, circular vendor financing and depreciation come from central banks and named investors; the counter-position is that revenue is now real and growing fast (Anthropic's reported H1 2026 revenue of $16.3B against $1.16B a year earlier).

### Inferences
- The numbers support "capital intensity without historical precedent, financed partly by suppliers", not "fraud". The net losses of tens of billions at Anthropic are mostly non-cash accounting charges; the operating loss is the figure to quote.
- The real exposure is fixed commitments: Anthropic's $518B and OpenAI's $1.4T are take-or-pay style obligations set against revenue one to two orders of magnitude smaller. That is the sober form of the bubble argument.
- For the audience: if financing tightens, the likely effect on users is higher prices and tighter limits (already visible in section 1), not disappearance of the technology.

## 3. Physical limits: energy, grid, chips, memory

### Takeaway
Physical constraints are measurable and already binding in three places: power equipment (gas turbines sold into 2031, transformer lead times above three years), advanced packaging (CoWoS still about 10-20% short in 2026), and memory (DRAM contract prices roughly doubling in Q1 2026, pushing 2026 PC and smartphone shipments down about 10-13%). They slow and reprice the build-out; they do not stop it, and the IEA still puts data centres at about 3% of world electricity in 2030.

### Cited Findings

Regional: US 224 TWh (2025) → 426 TWh (2030); China 117 → 277 TWh. [IEA Energy and AI, executive summary](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary), [IEA Key questions on Energy and AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary%C2%A0) (IEA pages returned 403 to direct fetch; numbers from search summaries of IEA pages and [PV Tech](https://www.pv-tech.org/ai-data-centre-demand-to-more-than-double-by-2030/)). ## 7. Reliability of vendor-published numbers

### Takeaway
There are documented cases where headline AI numbers proved soft: OpenAI's circulated $70B run-rate was reported by the FT as about $50B, run-rate and recognised revenue differ by about 2x at Anthropic for 2025, a CEO forecast of "90% of code in six months" did not materialise on schedule, and a controlled trial found developers 19% slower while believing they were 20% faster. Independent cross-checks exist (METR, Epoch, Stanford AI Index, audited S-1 figures), and the IPO filings improve disclosure.

### Cited Findings
- [SR] Axios, 2026-10-13, "News about AI startups' financials can be suspect": the FT reported OpenAI's run rate was about $50B in September 2026, roughly $20B below a $70B figure that had circulated and that came from investors extrapolating from Anthropic's performance; analyst Paul Kedrosky calls the metrics "inherently arbitrary and capricious". [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/13/openai-anthropic-revenue-finances) (403 on fetch; from search summary).
- [SR] Run-rate versus recognised revenue: Anthropic recognised $4.6B in 2025 while its year-end run-rate was about $9B; OpenAI's 2026 revenue is projected near $35B, about half its expected year-end run-rate. [Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/10/13/openai-anthropic-revenue-finances); the $4.6B is confirmed by S-1 coverage ([SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/09/29/leaked-anthropic-ipo-filing-reveals-8b-operating-loss-rapid-revenue-growth/)).
- [S] Bloomberg via PYMNTS, 2026-04-07: ARR is "one of the least-trusted" metrics in AI; it multiplies one month by 12; there are no audit requirements or SEC definitions for private firms (Chuck Eesley, Stanford); trial revenue is counted as recurring even when customers cancel. [PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/artificial-intelligence-2/2026/ai-firms-annual-recurring-revenue-metrics-draw-scrutiny/).

## 8. Intellectual property

### Takeaway
In the US, purely AI-generated output has no copyright, while human-directed and human-edited work can; training on lawfully acquired books was held fair use in Bartz v. Anthropic, which nonetheless ended in a $1.5B settlement over pirated copies. For code specifically, the Ninth Circuit ruled for GitHub, Microsoft and OpenAI on the DMCA claim in September 2026, but ordinary infringement and licence-contamination questions remain open. Germany's GEMA v. OpenAI went the other way on training.

### Cited Findings
- [SR] US Copyright Office, "Copyright and AI, Part 2: Copyrightability", 2025-01-29: human authorship is required; wholly AI-generated material is not protected; prompts alone generally do not suffice; human-made inputs perceptible in the output and human modifications can be protected; no new legislation recommended. [Copyright Alliance summary](https://copyrightalliance.org/ai-report-part-2-copyrightability/), [Akin Gump](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/ai-law-and-regulation-tracker/us-copyright-office-releases-part-2-of-ai-report). Copyright Alliance represents rights holders.
- [SR] Thaler v. Perlmutter: the US Supreme Court declined to hear the case in March 2026, leaving in place the rule that a work with no human author cannot be registered. Reported by a secondary guide; not confirmed from the docket. [secondary guide](https://tahoor.beehiiv.com/p/ai-copyright-law-2026-creators-guide).
- [SR] Bartz v. Anthropic: Judge Alsup held training on lawfully purchased, digitised books to be fair use but not the retention of pirated copies; the case settled for $1.5B, described as the largest copyright settlement in US history, and the court approved it. [Polsinelli](https://www.polsinelli.com/artificial-intelligence-machine-learning/publications/ai-vs-authors-update-court-approves-historic-anthropic-settlement-while-meta-litigation-continues). Law-firm client alert.
- [SR] Thomson Reuters v. Ross: the Third Circuit upheld the ruling against fair use, but the product was "not a generative AI", which limits its reach. [MLex](https://www.mlex.com/mlex/artificial-intelligence/articles/2535265).
- [SR] NYT v. OpenAI: on 2026-01-05 Judge Stein affirmed an order that OpenAI produce 20 million anonymised ChatGPT conversation logs; the case continues. [secondary](https://thelawreporters.com/ai-copyright-lawsuits-us-court-rulings-2026).
- [SR] GEMA v. OpenAI (Munich): described as the only ruling so far to reject the training defence outright; under appeal. A German decision against Suno followed. [secondary guide](https://tahoor.beehiiv.com/p/ai-copyright-law-2026-creators-guide), [Copyhype, 2026-07-31](https://www.copyhype.com/2026/07/fridays-endnotes-07-31-26/).
- [SR] Doe v. GitHub, Ninth Circuit, 2026-09-16 (No. 24-7700, Judge Miller for a unanimous panel): affirmed dismissal of the DMCA section 1202(b) claim; Copilot and Codex "generated new code that never contained CMI" (copyright management information) instead of stripping it from copies; the court did not decide whether training infringes, and ordinary copyright claims are untouched. [Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/09/ninth-circuit-rules-generative-ai-outputs-are-not-stripped-copies-under-the-dmca), [EFF](https://www.eff.org/tl/deeplinks/2026/09/victory-appeals-court-rejects-expansive-new-copyright-claim), [opinion PDF via Courthouse News](https://www.courthousenews.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/doe-vs-github-ninth-circuit.pdf) (PDF not converted in this run).
- [SR] Licence contamination: a vendor report (May 2026) says 68% of audited codebases contain licence conflicts and that AI-generated snippets make this harder to detect because scanners rely on declared dependencies; copyleft obligations can attach without the developer's knowledge. [HeroDevs](https://www.herodevs.com/blog-posts/68-of-codebases-contain-license-conflicts-and-ai-generated-code-is-making-it-worse), [SD Times](https://sdtimes.com/ai/report-open-source-licensing-conflicts-hit-an-all-time-high-as-organizations-struggle-to-audit-ai-generated-code-for-ip-risks/.). Vendor selling compliance tooling; treat the percentage as indicative.
- [SR] Indemnities: Microsoft's Copilot Copyright Commitment (2023-09-07) extends IP indemnity to paid commercial Copilot products, GitHub Copilot included, on condition that built-in guardrails and filters are used; Microsoft claims no rights in outputs; it does not settle whether the output is protectable at all. [Microsoft](https://blogs.microsoft.com/on-the-issues/2023/09/07/copilot-copyright-commitment/).
- [S] Russia: the 2026 AI law lets sovereign and national models train on lawfully obtained or openly accessible copyrighted works (from 2027-03-01); a broader copyright block was removed from the final text. [T-Zh](https://t-j.ru/news/duma-ai-law/), [ppc.world](https://ppc.world/news/gosduma-prinyala-v-pervom-chtenii-zakonoproekt-o-razvitii-ii-v-rossii/).

### Inferences
- Two separate risks should not be merged on a slide: (1) ownership, since code an agent wrote with little human shaping may not be protectable as the company's copyright in the US; (2) infringement, since generated code may reproduce licensed code. The first is settled in direction, the second is largely unlitigated.
- Indemnities cover paying commercial customers who keep filters on; they do not help users reaching models through grey-market resellers, which is the Russian case.

### Gaps
- Getty v. Stability (UK), US Copyright Office Part 3 (training), Anthropic/OpenAI/Google indemnity terms, and any Russian court practice on AI output: not retrieved.
- Several items above come from secondary legal commentary; court documents were not read.

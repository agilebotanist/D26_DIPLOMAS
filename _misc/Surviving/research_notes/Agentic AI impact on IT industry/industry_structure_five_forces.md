## 1. Market reaction: the 2026 software and IT services sell-off, pricing models, exposure, counter-view

### Takeaway
Between 12 January and 23 February 2026 the S&P Software and Services index lost about 25%, with Anthropic's Claude Cowork (12 January) and its 11 vertical plugins (30 January) named as the trigger; IT services followed in a second leg (Nifty IT down about a fifth in 2026, Accenture's record 18% one-day fall on 18 June 2026), and a partial rebound came in October 2026. The market priced a threat to seat-based pricing and labour-based billing, while analysts (Gartner, Bain, JP Morgan) describe a repricing and "metamorphosis" in which systems of record with proprietary data and embedded rules are the least exposed.

### Inferences
- The market treated two different business models as one trade: per-seat software and per-hour services. Both sell human attention units, which is what agents substitute.
- The trigger events were small (a blog post, 11 open-source plugins). The size of the reaction shows that investors were already doubting terminal value; it is evidence of perceived, not yet realised, structural change. Revenue at Accenture, Cognizant and IBM was still growing when the stocks fell.
- Analyst consensus on exposure: most exposed are horizontal, UI-heavy, per-seat tools for routine work (support tier 1, list building, task boards, invoice processing); least exposed are systems of record with proprietary data, regulatory logic and transaction embedding.

## 2. Threat of new entrants: AI-native tools, tiny teams, creation volumes (tests hypothesis a)

### Takeaway
Entry cost for software has collapsed in measurable ways: App Store submissions rose 84% year over year in Q1 2026, and AI coding tools reached multi-billion revenue run-rates in two to three years. Evidence against the strong form of the hypothesis: downloads grew only 2 to 3%, vibe-coding traffic and retention are weak, no one-person unicorn is documented, and the best controlled study of experienced developers could not establish a reliable speedup.

### Inferences
- Hypothesis (a) is supported for the step from idea to demo or first version: the volume of new apps and the revenue of the tools are hard evidence that many more people now ship something. It is weaker for the step from demo to a business: attention (downloads) did not grow, so the scarce resource moved from building to distribution and customer understanding. That is consistent with the second half of the hypothesis (advantage to those who understand customers).
- The fastest-growing "new entrants" are the tool makers themselves, not their users. The clearest winners of cheap software so far sell the shovels.
- METR's own difficulty (developers refuse the no-AI arm) is itself a signal of perceived value, but the measured effect on experienced developers in mature codebases remains uncertain.

## 3. Threat of substitutes and build versus buy: replacements, clones, cheap rewrites (tests hypothesis b)

### Takeaway
There are now several public cases where agents rebuilt a known, well-specified system at a cost of about $1,000 to $20,000 in tokens (a C compiler, a Next.js-compatible framework, a browser engine, a relicensed library), which supports the claim that code alone is a weak moat. Every case has the same caveats: an existing specification and test suite, expert human direction, non-production quality, and no evidence yet of ongoing maintenance; and the best-known "we replaced SaaS with AI" story (Klarna) was a vendor consolidation, not an LLM replacing Salesforce.

### Cited Findings
- CASE. Anthropic C compiler (5 Feb 2026, Nicholas Carlini): 16 parallel Claude agents, nearly 2,000 Claude Code sessions, about two weeks, just under $20,000 in API fees (about 2 billion input and 140 million output tokens), about 100,000 lines of Rust; builds a bootable Linux 6.9 on x86, ARM, RISC-V, compiles QEMU, FFmpeg, SQLite, PostgreSQL, Redis; about 99% on the GCC torture suite. Admitted limits: no 16-bit x86 backend (calls GCC), assembler and linker buggy (demo used GCC's), output slower than GCC with optimisation off, "not yet a drop-in replacement", GCC used as a known-good oracle during development. Source: [Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/building-c-compiler) [P, vendor showcase]. Critique that compilers are an unusually easy target (decades-old spec, full test suites, reference implementation): [Better Stack](https://betterstack.com/community/guides/ai/anthropic-ai-agents-c-compiler/) [S]
- CASE. Cloudflare vinext (Feb 2026): one engineer (an engineering manager directing AI), about one week, about $1,100 in tokens, over 800 sessions, re-implementation of about 94% of the Next.js 16 API surface on Vite; claimed builds 4.4x faster and client bundles 57% smaller. Caveats: labelled experimental by Cloudflare, Vercel reported security vulnerabilities, The Pragmatic Engineer judged it not production-ready, it reached beta 1 only about 20 weeks later, and the cost excludes the engineer's time. The target is an open-source framework whose commercial value sits in Vercel's hosting. Sources: [Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/vinext) [P, a Vercel competitor]; [InfoQ, March 2026](https://infoq.com/news/2026/03/cloudflare-vinext-experimental) [W]; [The Pragmatic Engineer](https://blog.pragmaticengineer.com/the-pulse-cloudflare-rewrites-next-js-as-ai-rewrites-commercial-open-source/) [S, independent]; [itwriting.com on beta 1](https://www.itwriting.com/blog/?p=12442) [S]
- CASE. Cursor FastRender browser (14 Jan 2026): agents running GPT-5.2 for about a week produced about 3 million lines (the count includes vendored code and fixtures). CEO Michael Truell: "It kind of works!", "very far from WebKit/Chromium parity". Critics: 88% CI job failure rate (Jason Gorman, Codemanship), "incredibly bloated", "definitely not a good browser engine" (Oliver Medhurst, ex-Mozilla); dispute over reuse of Servo components. Sources: [The Register, 2026-01-22](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/22/cursor_ai_wrote_a_browser/) [W]; [The Register opinion, 2026-01-26](https://www.theregister.com/2026/01/26/cursor_opinion/) [W]; [Software Improvement Group quality analysis](https://www.softwareimprovementgroup.com/blog/quality-of-fastrender/) [A]
- CASE. chardet 7.0.0 (March 2026): maintainer Dan Blanchard shipped an AI-assisted "ground-up" rewrite (Claude listed as contributor) of a library with about 130 million monthly PyPI downloads and changed the licence from LGPL 2.1 to MIT; original author Mark Pilgrim objected ("No right to relicense this project"). Bruce Perens argues the dispute shows AI will "kill software licensing". Sources: [The Register, 2026-03-06](https://www.theregister.com/2026/03/06/ai_kills_software_licensing/) [W]; [Phoronix](https://phoronix.com/news/chardet-llm-rewrite-relicense) [W]
- CASE. Cursor replaced the Sanity CMS with a custom git-based setup (Lee Robinson, Dec 2025), saving "tens of thousands of dollars" a year; Sanity replied that its collaboration features are not replicated. Source: [The Batch](https://www.deeplearning.ai/the-batch/claude-cowork-plugins-trigger-a-saas-stock-selloff-but-partnerships-lead-to-slight-rebound) [S]
- CASE. Klarna. August 2024 investor call: CEO Sebastian Siemiatkowski said "We just shut down Salesforce" and that Workday would follow. March 2025 clarification on X: "So no, we did not replace SaaS with an LLM"; Salesforce was replaced by an internal stack built on Neo4j (graph database) with interfaces built quickly using Cursor, and the aim was data unification, not licence savings. Klarna told CX Today it moved to other SaaS products and consolidated. Sources: [Diginomica, 2025-03-07](https://www.diginomica.com/those-shutting-down-salesforce-and-workday-rumors-klarna-no-we-didnt-replace-saas-llm-admits-ceo) [W]; [CX Today](https://www.cxtoday.com/crm/klarna-ceo-tremendously-embarrassed-by-salesforce-fallout-explains-what-really-happened/) [W]
- CASE. Airbnb test migration: about 3,500 React test files moved from Enzyme to React Testing Library in six weeks against an estimate of 1.5 years; automation reached 97% and about 100 files were finished by hand. Source: [Analytics India Magazine](https://analyticsindiamag.com/global-tech/airbnb-uses-llms-to-pull-off-an-18-month-enzyme-to-rtl-migration-in-just-6-weeks-1) [S, reports Airbnb's engineering post]
- Google internal migrations: an experience report describes LLM-assisted migrations judged against a target of at least 50% faster completion, done by "a handful of engineers" for work equal to several hundred engineers; results depend on Google's monorepo, tooling and internal model. Source: [arXiv 2501.06972](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.06972v1) [P]; [FSE 2025 industry paper](https://conf.researchr.org/details/fse-2025/fse-2025-industry-papers/14/Migrating-Code-At-Scale-With-LLMs-At-Google) [P]
- COBOL: Anthropic's 23 Feb 2026 post claims AI removes "the tasks that once made COBOL modernization cost-prohibitive" (exploration and analysis phases). IBM has offered COBOL-to-Java translation in watsonx Code Assistant since 2023, and AWS, Microsoft and Google have tools, without yet denting mainframe revenue. Source: [CIO.com](https://www.cio.com/article/4137185/anthropics-claim-that-ai-can-quickly-refactor-cobol-rattles-ibm-investors.html) [W]
- Nubank reported up to twelvefold efficiency in a large ETL migration (cited by BCG, origin is a Cognition Devin case). Source: [BCG, 2026-02-20](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2026/the-200-billion-dollar-ai-opportunity-in-tech-services) [A]

### Inferences
- The common precondition in all cheap-rebuild cases is a pre-existing oracle: a public specification, a reference implementation or a large test suite. Agents are cheap at reproducing behaviour that can be checked automatically. This links hypothesis (b) to hypothesis (e): where checking is cheap, copying is cheap.
- What was copied in each case is the code artefact. None of the cases copied the installed base, the hosting business, the support organisation, certification or the data.
- The chardet case shows a second effect: licences (copyleft, and by extension proprietary source licences) lose force when a functional equivalent can be regenerated. Copyright protects expression, not behaviour.
- Honest framing for the slide: "proof that it can be rebuilt" is established; "proof that the rebuilt thing was run in production and maintained for a year" is not.

### Gaps
- Amazon's Java upgrade claim (Andy Jassy, August 2024: about 4,500 developer-years and about $260M annual savings from Amazon Q upgrading to Java 17) is recalled from memory and was not re-verified because the search budget ended; find the original post before using.
- No verified case was found of a large enterprise cancelling a major SaaS contract and running an agent-built replacement in production at scale. Gartner notes most businesses do not plan to cancel CRM and ERP subscriptions ([Channel Dive](https://www.channeldive.com/news/software-agentic-arbitrage-saaspocalypse-gartner/824309/)).
- No independent audit of long-run maintenance cost of agent-written codebases was found.

## 4. Bargaining power of suppliers: model labs, Nvidia, labs moving up the stack

### Takeaway
The application layer depends on very few suppliers: Menlo Ventures puts Anthropic plus OpenAI at about 86% of enterprise LLM API spend in 2026, and the labs now sell directly competing applications (Claude Code, Codex, Cowork plugins). Wrapper economics are thin: Cursor was estimated at negative gross margins in 2025 and only "slightly" positive by April 2026, and the Windsurf episode shows that a supplier can cut off a customer within days.

## 7. Rivalry and moats: what remains defensible (tests hypothesis c)

### Takeaway
Analysts converge on a short list of durable advantages: proprietary data and systems of record with embedded regulatory logic, distribution and customer intimacy, earned trust and the right to operate in production, integration depth, and willingness to carry outcomes; code, model access and user interface are named as weak or temporary. ## 8. Liability and accountability (tests hypothesis d)

### Takeaway
Courts, tribunals and clients consistently hold the deploying organisation or the signing professional responsible for AI output: Air Canada (2024), Deloitte Australia (2025), the Replit incident (2025) and a growing tally of lawyers' cases all end with a named human or firm paying. Regulation is extending strict product liability to software while delaying AI-specific duties, and insurance for agent errors exists but is narrow.

### Cited Findings
- CASE. Air Canada (Moffatt v. Refund amounts differ by source (about A$97,000 in one report). Sources: [AP via CTV News](https://www.ctvnews.ca/business/article/deloitte-to-partially-refund-australian-government-for-report-with-apparent-ai-generated-errors/) [W]; [CFO Dive](https://www.cfodive.com/news/deloitte-refunds-60k-report-ai-errors-australian-government-accounting/) [W]

- In every case the argument "the AI did it" failed or was not even tried. Liability attaches to whoever deploys, signs or sells. This makes accountability a scarce, priceable resource and explains why outcome-based contracts and insurance appear at the same time.

- Professional licensing (lawyers, auditors, engineers, doctors) creates a human signature bottleneck that agents cannot remove; it caps substitution in licensed services.

## 9. Physical and hard-to-validate domains (tests hypothesis e)

### Takeaway
Evidence supports a later and slower effect where validation is physical: in 2026 academic tests, coding agents produced no strictly passing mechanical CAD part on first attempt when checked by finite element analysis, and medical-device and high-risk AI rules were being loosened or delayed, not removed. The main counter-example is chip design, where all three EDA vendors launched long-running agents in 2026, precisely because that field has deterministic simulators and sign-off tools.

### Cited Findings
- Mechanical CAD: in a 2026 preprint using FEA as feedback, the best configuration met about 20% of typed requirements on average, and Codex (GPT-5.5) and Claude Code (Opus 4.7) agents "do not produce a single strict-passing artifact" in the first-attempt sweep. Source: [arXiv 2605.17448](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.17448) [P, preprint]
- A second paper finds that simulation feedback exposes mechanical failures invisible to visual inspection; an IJCAI 2026 paper proposes embedding validated knowledge-based engineering tools in the agent loop ("physics in the loop"). Sources: [IJCAI 2026 proceedings](https://www.ijcai.org/proceedings/2026/714) [P]; [arXiv 2605.19717](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.19717) [P]
- Counter-example, chip design: at DAC 2026 Synopsys announced agentic workflows (vendor claim: up to 50x faster time to validated RTL versus its own non-agentic flow), Cadence added agents including for PCB and packaging, Siemens added self-verifying agents that check decisions against deterministic physics-based engines. Verdantix calls semiconductor design "ground zero" for AI-accelerated engineering while noting open questions of robustness, validation and trust. - Legacy systems are a half-physical case: IBM argues the hard part of COBOL modernisation is "everything the application runs on and integrates with", and mainframe revenue grew 48%. Source: [CIO.com](https://www.cio.com/article/4137185/anthropics-claim-that-ai-can-quickly-refactor-cobol-rattles-ibm-investors.html) [W]

### Inferences
- The explanatory variable is the cost and speed of the oracle. Software with tests and chips with simulators and sign-off tools give agents a fast, deterministic check; mechanical parts, multi-physics systems, clinical outcomes and buildings need slow, expensive or regulated physical validation. This unifies hypotheses (b) and (e).
- The hypothesis needs one refinement: "physical" is not the boundary, "cheaply verifiable" is. Chip design is physical but verifiable in simulation, so it moves early. Legacy enterprise systems are pure software but poorly specified, so they move slowly.
- Regulatory delay in the EU for embedded AI extends the period during which certified incumbents in machinery and devices face less agent-native competition.

### Gaps
- No industry adoption statistics were found for agents in embedded software, industrial automation (PLC), construction or multi-physics simulation.
- No retrieved 2026 source on AlphaFold-style science results or on robot deployment counts.
- Vendor speed claims in EDA (50x) have no independent replication.

### Inferences
- Honest split of causes, as far as the evidence goes. The largest part is the 2021 bubble: $17.5B was roughly 29 times today's ARR, a price no later market supported. The second part is rivalry and bundling by suites (Microsoft, Canva, Figma), which the source names explicitly. AI enters in a third, indirect way: the 2026 compression of SaaS multiples (section 1) lowers what any buyer pays, and Miro's price of about 2.3 times ARR on enterprise value (3 times on equity value; own arithmetic from the figures above) sits below the 4 times that TechCrunch calls normal for a healthy SaaS company. No source found quantifies these shares, and none says that customers rebuilt a whiteboard with agents.
- Miro is part of a pattern, not an outlier: Airtable (about 88% below peak on headline price, with ARR growing more than 20%) and Eventbrite (about 72% below IPO value) went to the same buyer within ten months. The caveat is that all three comparables involve one acquirer, so the pattern also describes Bending Spoons' strategy.
- Five forces placement. Rivalry: standalone collaboration tools lose to suites that bundle the function. Substitutes: the whiteboard is a UI-level, horizontal, per-seat product, the category that Bain and Gartner rate as most exposed (section 1). The strongest structural signal is the buyer's model: a 620-person company that runs acquired products with a small fraction of the original staff, with its CEO crediting AI. That shows software operation, not only creation, becoming cheap, and it shows that an installed base of paying customers keeps its value while the organisation around the code does not.
- Counter-reading for balance: a profitable company with $600M ARR and 100 million users was worth $1.8B in cash. The customer base and brand were what was bought, which supports hypothesis (c).

## 3. Bubble debate: named positions and capex

### Takeaway
By autumn 2026 the official warnings are specific and dated: the IMF lists an AI correction first among three risks and counts $650B of debt and $300B of equity exchanged for $1.4T of future revenue commitments; the Bank of England moved from "stretched valuations" (2025) to debt and "circular arrangements" (September 2026). The IMF's own staff also give the main counter-argument: price-to-earnings ratios have fallen because earnings keep beating forecasts, which is the difference from 2000.

### Cited Findings

IMF (publisher: intergovernmental lender; surveillance mandate)

## 6. The flattening side of the S-curve

### Takeaway
No named 2026 source shows that measured capability has flattened. The independent sceptical arguments are of three kinds: reliability improves far more slowly than accuracy (Kapoor and Narayanan), the headline metric overstates practical reach and its cost is rising (Ord, Witkin, METR's own notes), and diffusion is slow whatever the capability (Narayanan and Kapoor). The strongest reply is METR's November 2026 assessment that one lab's progress on well-specified tasks roughly doubled in pace, with the evidence largely redacted.

### Cited Findings

Slowing or limits
- [P] Sayash Kapoor (Princeton), "New Paper: Towards a science of AI agent reliability", 2026-02-24 (paper arXiv 2602.16666 per the post): "nearly two years of rapid capability progress have produced only modest reliability gains"; across 14 models, two benchmarks and 12 reliability dimensions, "reliability has improved only modestly over 18 months, while accuracy improved substantially". The authors call the findings "tentative". [AI as Normal Technology](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/new-paper-towards-a-science-of-ai). Academic authors with a book and newsletter built on the sceptical position.
- [P] Narayanan and Kapoor, "AI as Normal Technology", Knight First Amendment Institute, 2025-04-15: "transformative economic and societal impacts will be slow (on the timescale of decades)"; "the speed of diffusion is inherently limited by the speed at which not only individuals, but also organizations and institutions, can adapt to technology"; the argument "is applicable even if progress in AI methods can be arbitrarily sped up". Evidence: 40% of US adults used generative AI in August 2024 but for 0.5 to 3.5% of work hours. [Knight Institute](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/ai-as-normal-technology).
- [P] Arvind Narayanan, "Why AI hasn't replaced software engineers, and won't", 2026-06-11: "there is enough evidence to reject the narrative that once AI capabilities reach a certain threshold, it will cause mass layoffs." Evidence: New York WARN filings, 46 of about 25,000 laid-off workers (about 0.2%) attributed to AI; an HBR survey in which 21% of executives made large cuts "in anticipation of" AI against 2% tied to implementation. [AI as Normal Technology](https://www.normaltech.ai/p/why-ai-hasnt-replaced-software-engineers).
- [P] Toby Ord (Oxford), "Is there a Half-Life for the Success Rates of AI Agents?", 2025-05-07: under a constant failure rate the 80% horizon is about one third of the 50% horizon, the 90% horizon one seventh and the 99% horizon one seventieth. [Ord](https://www.tobyord.com/writing/half-life). His 2026 correction, "Hazard Rates for AI Agents Decline as a Task Goes On", 2026-02-04: "Contrary to my earlier hypothesis, AI agents probably don't have a constant hazard rate", and 99% horizons are "deeply uncertain" and probably shorter still. [Ord](https://www.tobyord.com/writing/hazard-rates-for-ai-agents-decline). Cite the pair, not the first alone.
- [P] Toby Ord, "Are the Costs of AI Agents Also Rising Exponentially?", 2025-12-22: "the METR trend is partly driven by unsustainably increasing inference compute"; from METR's charts, a human engineer costs about $120 an hour, GPT-5 about $13 an hour on 45-minute tasks but about $120 an hour on 2-hour tasks, and o3 about $350 an hour at its full horizon. He rates the evidence "moderate". [Ord](https://www.tobyord.com/writing/hourly-costs-for-ai-agents). Follow-up, 2026-10-09: on UK AISI data all models scale sub-linearly, so ten times the tokens buys less than ten times the horizon. [Ord](https://www.tobyord.com/writing/ai-scaling-vs-human-scaling).
- [P] Nathan Witkin, "Against the METR graph", Transformer, 2026-01-20: "It is impossible to draw meaningful conclusions from METR's Long Tasks benchmark." Evidence: human baselines from 140 engineers recruited through METR's network, about three per task, paid by the hour; repository maintainers were "5-18x faster" than baseliners. A validity critique, not a claim of flattening. [Transformer](https://www.transformernews.ai/p/against-the-metr-graph-coding-capabilities-software-jobs-task-ai).
- [P] METR's own cautions. ### Inferences
- The defensible statement for the talk: nobody has measured a plateau, the measuring instrument for long tasks is saturated and assumption-sensitive, and the two best-supported cautions concern reliability and cost per hour, which are what a business process needs.
- The two camps argue past each other. The acceleration evidence concerns well-specified, checkable tasks; the "normal technology" argument concerns adoption by organisations and holds, in its authors' words, even if capability accelerates. Both can be true, which matches the "two S-curves" inference in the analysts file.
- The 2025 "developers 19% slower" result should no longer be quoted as current. METR's own position is that developers are probably faster now and that it cannot say by how much; the perception-gap finding of 2025 remains valid as a dated result.
- Sceptics with a stake (Marcus, LeCun) and accelerationists with a stake (AI Futures, lab-commissioned assessments) should be labelled symmetrically.

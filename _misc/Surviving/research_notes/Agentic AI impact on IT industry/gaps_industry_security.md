## 1. Published strategy analyses and primary texts of investor essays (five forces gap, sections 7 and 10)

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed or management-review article that applies Porter's five forces or Helmer's 7 Powers specifically to software or IT services under agentic AI was found; the nearest items are one generic HBR article (resource-based view, 2024), one unreviewed SSRN preprint (September 2026, abstract only) and a Fortune five forces exercise on the model labs. The talk's five forces table therefore remains original synthesis. The primary texts of Stratechery, a16z, Sequoia and Bain were read and agree on one point (code and user interface stop being defensible) and disagree on where control moves: to the system of record (Bain), to an orchestration or agent layer above it (a16z, Stratechery), or to whoever sells the finished work (Sequoia).

### Cited Findings

Strategy frameworks

- HBR, Jay B. Barney (University of Utah) and Martin Reeves (BCG), "AI Won't Give You a New Sustainable Advantage", September-October 2024 issue. Framework: resource-based view, generic, not software-specific. Core claims: "gen AI will be more likely to erode a competitive advantage than to confer one, because its very nature makes new insights and data patterns almost immediately transparent to anyone using gen AI tools"; "Value is created but not captured, at least not for long" (the original uses a dash); advantage survives only "If your assets are rare and difficult for others to imitate"; "the only ones that will actually win with it will be those that can apply it to amplify the advantages they already have." On proprietary data they are sceptical: two firms' different datasets "might be very similar" in the patterns they contain. Source: [HBR](https://hbr.org/2024/09/ai-wont-give-you-a-new-sustainable-advantage) [P; Reeves's employer sells AI strategy work]
- SSRN preprint 7438718, Surajit Majumder, "Porter's Five Forces in the Age of Generative and Agentic AI: A Structural Reassessment of Competitive Strategy", dated 9 September 2026; no affiliation, no peer review, about 120 downloads. Only the abstract could be read. It is generic (software is not named). Claims: "the analytical structure of the framework remains valid, the empirical weight and directionality of several forces have shifted materially since 2023"; "Agentic AI exerts a systematically larger structural effect than GenAI alone on every force, most acutely on buyer bargaining power and competitive rivalry, because it automates execution rather than merely content production." The author describes the method as "a qualitative, practitioner-informed synthesis". Source: [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=7438718) [P, abstract only; full text blocked]
- Fortune (Geoff Colvin, 22 December 2025) applies the five forces to the model labs, not to software users, with Forrester's Charlie Dai and Gartner's Arun Chandrasekaran as sources. Per force: new entrants weak ("a three-horse race" of OpenAI, Google, Anthropic; barriers are "compute cost, talent scarcity, and regulatory complexity"); supplier power "strong" (chips, and data owners "now demanding money"); buyer power "moderate to strong" ("buyers are increasingly using multiple models and finding they can be compatible"); substitutes "medium and getting stronger" (small models in specific domains); rivalry "strong and getting much, much stronger". Useful for the supplier-power slide: it describes the industry that supplies the software industry. Source: [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/12/22/how-the-openai-vs-google-battle-looks-through-the-lens-of-michael-porters-5-forces-analysis/) [S]
- Hamilton Helmer in his own words (Lenny's Podcast, May 2024 per search snippet; transcript is machine-generated): "My own view currently, I don't see any change in seven powers from it in terms of an eighth power or something." He frames scale economies ("a fixed cost of trying to develop a model of a billion dollars or something"), network economies ("will models develop so that they learn in a way that for one user's interaction helps another user's interaction. That would be a powerful network economy") and switching costs ("if it learns about you ... then that's a switching cost") as open questions, and expects the largest effect on companies that use the technology, not on those that make it: generative AI's "biggest impact will be that tertiary class". He compares adoption to electricity reconfiguring the factory floor. He does not say which powers AI weakens. Sources: [transcript](https://podscripts.co/podcasts/lennys-podcast-product-career-growth/business-strategy-with-hamilton-helmer-author-of-7-powers) [P, auto-transcript]; [episode page](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/business-strategy-with-hamilton-helmer) [P]

### Inferences
- Per-force reading of the essays. Rivalry: up (Thompson: every vendor can now write "infinite software"). Supplier power: up (Thompson's "arms dealers"; Fortune rates rivalry among those suppliers as strong, which is the one brake on their pricing power). Buyer power: up through multi-model use and through agents that hide the application. Substitutes: the substitute for a software seat is labour done by an agent, sold as an outcome (Sequoia, a16z). New entrants: a16z itself says distribution "is the default winner", which tempers the entrant story even from a challenger investor.
- The defensibility debate reduces to one question the audience can hold: who owns the context? Bain says the system of record (data plus regulatory logic), a16z says the orchestration layer that accumulates "institutional context", Thompson says the single agent that knows the user. All three name context and distribution; none names code.
- Barney and Reeves give the academic backing for hypothesis (c): a widely available technology amplifies existing rare assets and does not create new ones. Helmer's own view is close: no eighth power, and the main effect lands on users of the technology.

Brave search was rate-limited after the first queries, so absence is weakly established; a ResearchGate item "Revising Porter's Forces in the Age of AI" (October 2025) was seen only as a snippet [SR].

## 2. Miro sold to Bending Spoons: closing, explanations, origin (five forces gap, section 12)

### Takeaway
The deal closed on 26 October 2026 at the announced terms (enterprise value $1.355 billion, equity value about $1.79 billion, with about $295 million of proceeds rolled into Bending Spoons shares). Every independent source read explains the price by 2021 multiples, the end of pandemic demand and competition from suites; none names AI as a cause. AI appears only in the buyer's description of its own method. Miro's origin in Perm in 2011 as RealtimeBoard is confirmed.

### Cited Findings
- Announcement, 10 September 2026: "definitive agreement to acquire Miro at an enterprise value of $1.355 billion. Together with Miro's net cash, this implies an equity value of approximately $1.79 billion." Miro figures in the release: "around $600 million in annual recurring revenue, nearly 90% from business and enterprise customers", about 4 million paying users, more than 750 customers above $100,000 ARR, 250,000 organisations, more than 100 million users. Khusid: "In Bending Spoons, Miro has an owner who invests for the long term and cares deeply about product quality." Source: [Bending Spoons](https://investors.bendingspoons.com/newsroom/bending-spoons-completes-the-acquisition-of-miro) [P]; repeated by [Dealroom](https://dealroom.co/news/167190-bending-spoons-closes-1-355b-miro-buyout-its-second-big-workplace-deal-i/) [S]

Source: [Bending Spoons](https://investors.bendingspoons.com/newsroom/bending-spoons-agrees-to-acquire-miro) [P]

No AI cause. Source: [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/josipamajic/2026/09/10/bending-spoons-buys-miro-at-90-discount-as-saas-unicorns-sell-for-scraps/) [S, contributor not staff]

### Inferences
- For the slide: Miro is a case of rivalry and bundling plus repricing, and the honest sentence is "nobody independent says AI did this". The AI link is on the buyer's side: an operating model that runs acquired products with far fewer people, which is what makes a 2.3 times ARR price attractive to this buyer.
- The $295 million rollover means some Miro investors chose shares in the consolidator over cash, a small signal that they see more value in the operating model than in the standalone product.

## 4. Liability: EU product liability, AI Act timing, 2026 decisions (five forces gap, section 8)

### Takeaway
The revised Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 is confirmed at the primary text: software, including software supplied as a service and AI systems, is a product under no-fault liability for products placed on the market after 9 December 2026, three weeks after the talk. The AI Act delay is now law (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026). The clearest 2026 court decision found is from Munich: a company is directly liable for false statements its AI system generates.

### Cited Findings
- Directive (EU) 2024/2853: "Done at Strasbourg, 23 October 2024"; published in the Official Journal on 18 November 2024; member states transpose "by 9 December 2026" (Article 22); it does "not apply to products placed on the market or put into service before 9 December 2026". Article 4(1): "'product' means all movables ... it includes electricity, digital manufacturing files, raw materials and software". Recital 13: "software is a product for the purposes of applying no-fault liability, irrespective of ... whether the software is stored on a device, accessed through a communication network or cloud technologies, or supplied through a software-as-a-service model", and "A developer or producer of software, including AI system providers within the meaning of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 ... should be treated as a manufacturer." Source: [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402853) [P]
- Limits and extensions in the same text. Open source: the directive "does not apply to free and open-source software that is developed or supplied outside the course of a commercial activity" (Article 2(2)). Updates: the manufacturer stays liable for defects arising after sale from "software, including software updates or upgrades" or from "a lack of software updates or upgrades necessary to maintain safety" where within its control (Article 11(2)), although "This Directive does not impose any obligation to provide updates or upgrades" (recital 51). Self-learning: "a manufacturer that designs a product with the ability to develop unexpected behaviour should remain liable" (recital 32). Evidence: courts can order disclosure, and defect or causation can be presumed where the defendant withholds evidence or technical complexity makes proof excessively difficult (Articles 9 and 10). Damage: death, injury including psychological harm, property, and "destruction or corruption of data that are not used for professional purposes" (Article 6); property and data used professionally are excluded. Source: [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=OJ:L_202402853) [P]
- EU AI Act timing: the Digital Omnibus on AI was adopted as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, signed 8 July 2026, published 24 July 2026, in force 27 July 2026. High-risk obligations now apply from "2 December 2027" for stand-alone Annex III systems and 2 August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products. Most Article 50 transparency duties applied from 2 August 2026, with a grace period to 2 December 2026 for marking content from systems already on the market. Source: [White and Case, 2026-08-04](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/eu-ai-omnibus-enters-force-amending-ai-act) [S, law firm]. This confirms the dates in the existing notes and closes the "formal adoption" gap.
- The separate AI Liability Directive proposal was withdrawn by the Commission in 2025 (IAPP, 12 February 2025; Euronews, 31 July 2025) [SR].
- Munich Regional Court I, 12 June 2026 (date inferred from a report of that day): in suits by two publishers the court held Google directly liable for false statements in AI Overviews that linked them to "subscription traps and fraudulent schemes", ordered it to stop and to bear 80% of costs; not final, Google will appeal. Reasoning: the overview is "a self-contained statement with independently comprehensible content", in the system's own words, so it is attributable to the operator and not a neutral list of links. Source: [Deutsche Welle](https://www.dw.com/en/german-court-holds-google-liable-for-fake-ai-answers/a-77527661) [S]
- Mobley v. Workday (N.D. California, Judge Rita Lin), order of about 22 June 2026: claims under California's fair employment law and one disability claim against the vendor of an AI screening tool go forward; the claim that Workday is liable as an employer and a race disparate-impact claim were dismissed; a collective action was certified in May 2025. Workday: "Our customers maintain full control of their hiring processes". No ruling on the merits. Source: [HR Dive, 2026-06-23](https://www.hrdive.com/news/workday-california-AI-bias-lawsuit-feha/823555/) [S]
- Amazon v. Perplexity (Comet shopping agent): a district court order blocking the agent (CNBC, 10 March 2026) was overturned on appeal (Reuters, 4 August 2026, "Amazon loses US court ban on Perplexity's AI shopping tools") [SR]. This is about whether a platform can exclude a user's agent, not about who pays for an agent's error.

### Inferences
- From 9 December 2026 the liability picture in the EU is asymmetric in a way that fits hypothesis (d): AI-specific duties are postponed to 2027 and 2028, the dedicated AI liability proposal is gone, and general strict product liability now covers software and AI providers. The message for business listeners: the rules that bite first are the old ones, extended.
- The directive protects consumers and private data; business-to-business losses stay in contract law. That is where outcome-based contracts and indemnities (main file, sections 5 and 6) do the work.
- The exclusion for non-commercial open source, with liability for commercial integrators, pushes accountability onto whoever packages and sells, which is again the party with a balance sheet.
- Munich and Mobley point the same way from two legal systems: output generated in the system's own words is the operator's statement, and a vendor's tool acting in a customer's process does not automatically shield the vendor.

### Gaps
- Whether member states transposed the directive on time: only headlines of law-firm trackers were seen (Faegre Drinker progress report, 30 October 2026; Linklaters on Belgium, 13 August 2026; Hogan Lovells on the Netherlands, 11 November 2026) [SR].
- The Munich case number and judgment text were not found; the date is inferred. The DW report is the only source read.
- No 2026 regulator fine (FTC, Garante, ICO, FCA) for an agent's action and no record-size lawyer sanction beyond those in the main file were found. No ruling yet assigns liability for an autonomous coding or customer-service agent's action after Air Canada.
- Council and Parliament press releases on the Omnibus were not read; the adoption data rest on one law-firm note.

### Inferences
- "CVE explosion" is accurate for the catalogue and misleading for risk. Three things are mixed in the count: backfilled and newly catalogued advisories (GitHub, VulnCheck, WordPress plugin authorities), organisational choices such as the Linux kernel assigning identifiers to most fixes since 2024, and genuinely new discovery, some of it AI-assisted. Only the third says anything about AI, and nobody has measured its share.
- Timing supports a partial AI role without proving it: the steepest rise (July to October 2026) follows the widening of restricted cyber models in June and coincides with GitHub's reported mid-2026 jump in agent-driven activity. Gamblin's cataloguing explanation was written on data to April and cannot cover the later months. State this as an open question.
- The processing side is the durable story for the talk: the public enrichment service now fully analyses about a third of new records by design, the exploited share is falling toward 1%, and time to exploit is negative. The scarce skill is prioritisation, which is the security file's thesis.
- Governance was fragile and is now steadier but opaque: one contract option in April 2025 stood between the industry and a lapse of its shared identifier system; in 2026 funding is secured on terms the board itself has not seen.

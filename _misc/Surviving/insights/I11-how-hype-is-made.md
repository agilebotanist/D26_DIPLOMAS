# I11. How hype is made, and how to read a claim

Theme file for the talk "Agentic AI impact on the IT industry". Sources: `research_notes/Agentic AI impact on IT industry/` (mainly `telegram_chat_leads.md`, `benchmarks_vs_reality.md` including section 10, `gaps_benchmarks.md`, `analysts_adoption_hype.md`, `gaps_economics_analysts.md`, `user_supplied_sources.md`, `security_agents.md`, `labour_market_skills.md`, `gaps_labour.md`), read on 2026-11-17. Where a later notes file (the `gaps_*` files and `user_supplied_sources.md`) corrects an earlier one, the later value is used and the correction is recorded in item 6 of the insight. Verification levels in brackets are those of `references_keymap.tsv`.

Framing for the whole file: nobody in these stories needs to be lying. Each party says the true things that suit it, measures what it can sell, and stays silent on the rest. The listener's job is to know which way a source leans, in both directions. The Telegram channel is described throughout as "a Russian-language AI news channel the speaker follows"; neither the channel nor its author is named, and its messages are cited by message id and date only.

## Five-line summary

1. The one thing to remember: every loud AI claim has an author with a reason to make it. The reason does not make the claim false, it tells you which way it leans, and the claims worth most are the ones a source makes against its own interest.
2. The best story: in October 2025 a vendor executive announced that a model had solved ten open Erdős problems; the mathematician who keeps the list answered that the model had found old papers; the post was gone within a day. Within a year AI proofs were real, and Fields Medallists were asking the labs to slow down. The hype was wrong on the day and right on the direction.
3. The best picture: one bar chart of US job cuts that employers attributed to AI, month by month in 2026. It climbs to 38,579 in May and falls to 1,265 in October. A technology does not come and go in five months; a way of wording a press release does.
4. The best question for discussion: think of the last AI claim you repeated to a colleague. Who said it first, what do they sell, and did anyone other than the author check it?
5. What the speaker should not claim: that vendors lie or that the hype is fake (the verified cases are about framing and definitions, and several hyped claims came true); that "95% of AI pilots fail" or "80% of AI projects fail"; that the channel's ratio of hopeful to doubtful posts is a measurement (one rater, 160 posts); and nothing in Bruce Schneier's or Sam Altman's exact words, because those two quotes could not be verified at source.

## I11.1 One feed, read every day, told four hopeful stories for every doubtful one

1. Title as claim. A single information diet tilts what you believe without a single false post: in the AI news channel the speaker follows, about one substantive post in three pushes capability and replacement, about one in fourteen doubts, and the best-known negative evidence never appears.

2. Number or data series. All figures are from the profile of the channel in `telegram_chat_leads.md` (export of 4,223 messages, 2020-11 to 2026-06-06). They are counts of what a channel says, never evidence about AI.
   - Hand classification of two random samples, 70 + 90 = 160 posts out of about 2,660 substantive posts (150+ characters) in 2025-01 to 2026-06: capability-boosting or "replacement is inevitable" 49 (31%); sceptical or critical 11 to 12 (7%); technical how-to 63 (39%); other 36 (23%).
   - Extrapolated: roughly 800 boosting posts against roughly 190 sceptical ones, a ratio near 4 to 1. One rater, wide margin.
   - Of 14 posts on the "bubble" read in 2025-2026, about 10 argue there is none and 3 concede a possible strong correction.
   - Never mentioned in the whole export: Klarna, Jevons, DORA, GitClear, the MIT NANDA report, Builder.ai, "workslop", Shopify. The 2025 randomised trial that found experienced developers slower with AI [@becker2025measuring] (primary) is never mentioned, although a survey paper attached to the channel cites it on its first page [@arxiv2025survey] (primary).
   - Where the feed comes from, by linked domain in 2025-2026: arxiv.org 168, x.com 65, businessinsider.com 60, github.com 49, huggingface.co 45. Preprints, vendor posts and business press, with commentary on top.
   - Volume: 575 messages in 2024, 1,441 in 2025, 1,619 in the first five months of 2026.
   - Chart: two bars, 31% against 7%, with a third, empty bar labelled "never mentioned" and the eight names under it. The empty bar is the picture.

3. Quotes (Russian originals checked by exact match inside the cited message; see the verification record for why the script cannot check Cyrillic).
   - "я эти песни неудачников в применении ИИ в разработке слышу постоянно" (message 2562, 2025-12-10). Gloss: "I hear these losers' songs about using AI in development all the time." Context: an answer to studies showing at most a 20% gain on legacy code. Speaker: the channel's author, a consultant who sells training in AI-assisted development and promotes his own method. Local file: `tg_2025-H2.md`. Result: EXACT.
   - "Контрольные группы НЕ догма и НЕ являются обязательным научным критерием" (message 2130, 2025-09-17). Gloss: "Control groups are NOT dogma and are NOT a required scientific criterion." Same speaker and interest. Local file: `tg_2025-H2.md`. Result: EXACT.

4. Example or case. On 2025-10-08 (message 2268) the channel takes a chart of benchmark trend lines that saturate around 2027 and concludes that by then agents can replace "на 100% текущих инженеров" (100% of current engineers) under an operator, and that "старой IT реальности осталось жить 2 года" (the old IT reality has two years to live). Both fragments: `tg_2025-H2.md`, EXACT. A benchmark curve becomes a labour forecast in three sentences, and the post is signed off by the author's view of his own clients. The same pattern recurs: a competitive-programming rating read as proof that seniors are outclassed, vendor CEOs relayed with agreement, layoffs attributed to AI by default, dates for AGI passed on without tracking earlier misses, critics dismissed instead of their evidence.

5. Counter-evidence, in proportion. The bias is selective, not total, and this matters for fairness. The same author is sharp on vendor marketing: "этот SWE Bench сломался, несите другой" ("this SWE-bench is broken, bring another one", message 2203, 2025-09-30, `tg_2025-H2.md`, EXACT), written five months before OpenAI said much the same in public (I11.3). He notes that a vendor's headline score rests on "multiple parallel attempts", that a tool vendor's story of agents building a browser on their own produced no serious application, and that a large layoff happened "не потому, что ИИ боты будут делать их работу" ("not because AI bots will do their work", message 3578, 2026-03-10, `tg_2026-H1.md`, EXACT) but to free money for AI spending. A crude word count gives a more balanced picture (256 "boost only" posts against 228 containing a sceptic word), but the sceptic words mostly appear in order to be dismissed. And the mirror image exists: a reader who followed only committed sceptics in 2025 would still be quoting "developers are 19% slower", which the study's own authors no longer treat as current [@metr2026changing] (primary).

6. Speaker-note material. Provenance: script profile of the whole export, about 20 keyword passes, roughly 350 messages read, two random samples classified by hand. Caveats: one rater; the discussion chat behind the channel is not in the export; no claim in the channel was checked online in that pass. Interest: the author sells courses and consulting and addresses IT managers and leads; posts regularly end in a pointer to his training. That is an ordinary business, and it explains the lean without any bad faith: a feed that sold "wait and see" would sell fewer courses. The channel also reverses itself without saying so (a lab described as near decline in 2025 becomes the main proof that there is no bubble in 2026). Slide rule from the brief: the channel is a list of leads, never evidence.

7. Confidence and theses. High on the direction of the tilt and on the absences (they are exact string searches). Low on the exact ratio. Broadens T7: the incentive to inflate is not limited to vendors ahead of an IPO, it reaches the commentators who relay them. Supports T3 through the author's own sober posts on benchmarks.

8. Question for the audience. Name one thing that is never mentioned in the feed you read every morning. If you cannot, how would you know?

9. Personal angle. From the brief: the speaker uses these tools daily for office, admin, teaching and research work, and treats this channel as a source of leads with a likely lean, not as evidence. One plain sentence: "I read a channel like this every day, and it took a count to show me what it never tells me." Speaker to supply: a moment when something he had picked up from the feed met his own work and did not hold, or held better than he expected.

## I11.2 The announcement was wrong on the day and right within the year

1. Title as claim. The October 2025 "solved Erdős problems" posts were a literature search announced as a breakthrough, and they were withdrawn in a day; by late 2026 AI proofs accepted by leading mathematicians were real. Inflated on the day is not the same as false in direction.

2. Number or data series. A timeline, chart-ready as dated points with "what was claimed" above the line and "what was shown" below it.
   - 2025-10-17/18: a vendor vice-president posts that a model "found solutions to 10 (!)" open Erdős problems and made progress on 11 others. Shown: retrieval of existing papers. Post deleted within a day [@techcrunch2025openaifalse] (secondary), [@decoderopenai] (secondary).
   - 2025-11-20: the sober version appears as a paper that presents literature search as one of the real uses [@bubeck2025early] (primary, bibliographic record).
   - 2026-01-04: two hobbyists with a public model resolve Problem 728, formally checked; described as the first largely autonomous resolution [@quantamagazine2026erdos] (primary).
   - January 2026: a 24-person vendor team runs a model over 700 "open" problems: 4 newly solved, 9 forgotten solutions found. May 2026: an agent resolves 9 of 353 formally stated problems [@quantamagazine2026erdos]. That is a hit rate of about 1 to 3%.
   - 2026-05-20: a counterexample to a 1946 conjecture by Erdős, vetted by nine mathematicians [@quantamagazine2026erdos].
   - Community tally frozen on 2026-06-30, hand-counted and approximate: 61 "AI standalone" entries, of which 19 full solutions, 22 partial and 10 incorrect; 84 entries that are literature search; 185 that are formalisation [@github2026aicontributions] (primary).
   - 2026-09-11: 25 Fields Medallists publish a declaration that starts from the premise that models can now solve major open problems [@tao2026severe] (primary).
   - 2026-10-06: a quasi-Riemann hypothesis proof, one version formally verified, which experts largely accept as correct; in the same release, hundreds of papers that the vendor's own staff had not all read, 3 withdrawn within a day and 14 revised [@quantamagazine2026openai] (primary), [@wikipedia2026openai] (secondary).

3. Quotes.
   - "GPT-5 found solutions to 10 (!) previously unsolved Erdős problems and made progress on 11 others." Kevin Weil, vice-president at OpenAI, in a since-deleted post, as quoted in [@techcrunch2025openaifalse]. Role and interest: executive of the model vendor. Local file: `converted/web/i11_techcrunch_erdos.md`. Result: VERIFIED (against the press report; the original post no longer exists).
   - "GPT-5 found references, which solved these problems, that I personally was unaware of." Thomas Bloom, mathematician, University of Manchester, who maintains the Erdős problems website; he called the announcement "a dramatic misrepresentation". Role and interest: independent academic, no product. Same source and file. Result: VERIFIED (both strings).
   - For the other half of the story: "I would have recommended acceptance without any hesitation. No previous AI-generated proof has come close to that." Tim Gowers, Fields Medallist, on the May 2026 counterexample [@quantamagazine2026erdos]. Role and interest: independent academic. Local file: `converted/web/i11_quanta_erdos.md`. Result: VERIFIED.

4. Example or case. The whole story is the case, and it has four actors worth naming aloud. The executive who announced. The one domain expert who knew that "open" on his own website only meant that he personally had not seen a solution. The competitors, who mocked the post in public, with their own interest in doing so. And the vendor's own researchers, who a month later published the careful version. The correction did not come from the vendor's checks; it came from one person who happened to own the list.

5. Counter-evidence, in proportion. This is the main place where the hyped claim later turned out right, and the talk should say so plainly. By autumn 2026 the dispute among mathematicians is no longer whether models can do it; it is about checking, readability, credit and purpose. The declaration of the Fields Medallists says that solving problems "is only a tool and proxy for achieving the primary goal of conceptual understanding and insight" (wording checked only against Terence Tao's own summary page [@taoterence], `converted/web/I_tao_ai_views.md`, VERIFIED there; the declaration page itself could not be fetched). Still, the inflation habit did not go away with the capability: early announcements, a bulk release that staff had not fully read, and more than 150 professors warning governments that firms have a commercial reason to overstate [@afp2026leiden] (primary). Both things are true at once.

6. Speaker-note material. Provenance: section 10 of `benchmarks_vs_reality.md`; `gaps_benchmarks.md` leaves mathematics out of scope, so nothing is corrected. Caveats: the October 2025 episode rests on press reports of deleted posts; counts in the community tally are hand-tallied; the October 2026 bulk release rests on one magazine article and a Wikipedia summary, and the counts differ between sources. Interests: the vendor sells the model; the competitors who mocked sell rival models; Quanta is funded by a foundation and editorially independent; Bloom and Gowers sell nothing. Mathematics is the best case for AI because a proof can be checked mechanically, so a 1 to 3% hit rate is affordable there and nowhere else. Theme I4 carries that argument; here the point is only the life cycle of a claim.

7. Confidence and theses. High on the October 2025 facts and on the 2026 results. Medium on any count. Corrects the "inflated, therefore untrue" reading of T3 and T7: the same company produced the false announcement and the real result eleven months apart.

8. Question for the audience. When a claim you laughed at a year ago turns out true, do you go back and change what you said about it?

9. Personal angle. The brief gives no first-person material on mathematics. One plain sentence: "The first announcement was wrong, the people who laughed were also wrong, and it took a year to find out which way." Speaker to supply: a claim about these tools that he dismissed, or believed, and later had to revise.

## I11.3 Vendors mark their own homework, and the most useful thing they publish is the correction

1. Title as claim. In 2026 the company that promoted the two best-known coding benchmarks disowned both within five months. Vendor claims are often real and still cannot be checked from outside; the statements to trust most are the ones a vendor makes against itself.

2. Number or data series.
   - First yardstick. State of the art on SWE-bench Verified moved from 74.9% to 80.9% in six months. In February 2026 OpenAI audited 138 hard problems and found material flaws in 59.4% of them, and found that every frontier model it tested could reproduce the original human fix from memory. It stopped reporting the score [@openai2026swebench] (primary).
   - Second yardstick. On the replacement, SWE-bench Pro, the frontier pass rate rose from 23.3% to 80.3% in eight months. In July 2026 OpenAI estimated that about 30% of its tasks are broken (200 of 731 by one method, 249 by another) and withdrew its recommendation [@openai2026separating] (primary).
   - What a pass is worth. Project maintainers would merge about 24 percentage points fewer AI patches than the automatic grader accepts [@metr2026manyswe] (primary).
   - "Share of code written by AI" at Google: more than a quarter (October 2024), 50% (autumn 2025), 75% (April 2026), in each case "approved by engineers" [@pichai2024q3earnings] (primary), [@pichai2026cloud] (primary). Microsoft: "maybe 20%, 30%" in some repositories, April 2025, hedged and not updated [@cnbc2025nadella] (secondary). Anthropic: between 70% and 90% (January 2026) [@fortune2026anthropic] (secondary); "Over 90% of the changes merged into Anthropic's core codebase are now written by Claude (with human guidance)." (November 2026) [@anthropic2026claude] (primary; `converted/web/C_anth_fable52.md`, VERIFIED). No shared definition.
   - Chart: two short lines, each rising steeply and each ending in a cross labelled "withdrawn by its sponsor", February and July 2026.

3. Quotes.
   - "we retract our earlier recommendation to adopt SWE-Bench Pro." OpenAI, July 2026 [@openai2026separating]. Role and interest: model vendor and co-creator of the earlier benchmark; the interest cuts both ways, because the retraction also discredits a board on which competitors led. Local file: `converted/web/openai_signal_noise.md`. Result: VERIFIED.
   - "Today, 75% of all new code at Google is now AI-generated and approved by engineers, up from 50% last fall." Sundar Pichai, chief executive of Google, at the company's cloud conference, 2026-04-22 [@pichai2026cloud]. Role and interest: head of a company that sells models, cloud capacity and coding tools. Local file: `converted/web/A_google_next2026.md`. Result: VERIFIED.
   - Backstage, the best one-line caveat comes from a vendor chief executive himself: "“Writing all of the code” and “doing the task of a software engineer end to end” are very different things" (Dario Amodei, essay footnote, January 2026 [@amodei2026adolescence], `converted/web/A_amodei_adolescence.md`, VERIFIED). Also verified and available: OpenAI's sentence that improvements on the first benchmark "increasingly reflect how much the model was exposed to the benchmark at training time" [@openai2026swebench], `converted/web/openai_swebench_verified.md`.

4. Example or case. Two security stories show the same shape outside benchmarks. In November 2025 Anthropic reported an espionage campaign against about 30 targets that it described as 80 to 90% executed by its own model [@anthropic2025gtgespionage] (secondary). Security researchers doubted it in public for one plain reason: no technical indicators were published, so nobody outside the company could check [@bleepingcomputeranthropicclaim] (secondary). In April 2026 the same company announced a model that had found thousands of serious software flaws and withheld it from general release [@anthropic2026project] (primary). Bruce Schneier, the most cited independent voice in the field, described the launch as a public-relations move that worked, while agreeing that the underlying problem is real [@schneier2026anthropic] (primary in the keymap; wording not verified locally, see "Unverified, keep off slides").

5. Counter-evidence, in proportion. In both security stories the direction was later supported by others. A different vendor's threat team reported in May 2026 the first exploit it assessed as built with AI help [@googlethreat2026gtigfirst] (primary; also an interested party). For the withheld model, a government institute ranked it first in its own cyber evaluation [@ukaisecurity2026evaluation] (primary), and Mozilla shipped fixes for 271 flaws found with it [@arstechnica2026mozilla] (secondary). Against the scale of the claim: by early November 2026 a public ledger showed 37,966 logged findings and 217 marked fixed [@mallorypatrick] (secondary). And the share-of-code statements may well be accurate as stated; what they count is approved keystrokes of new code, which is not delivered value and not headcount. So the fair summary is "real, framed, and hard to verify from outside", not "invented".

6. Speaker-note material. Provenance: both OpenAI posts and both Google statements were read at source; the espionage report and its critics were read through secondary coverage only; the fetch of the critics' article failed in this pass. Corrections from later notes: `gaps_benchmarks.md` supplies the exact wording of the share-of-code statements, which the main file had held from a tracker and from memory. Caveat on the benchmark numbers: the 80.3% on the second benchmark is the vendor's own harness and never appeared on the benchmark owner's public board. Interest: a vendor benefits when its benchmark number is high, and also when a rival's lead on that benchmark is discredited; a vendor that withholds a model benefits from the fear and from the policy position. An earlier case of the same kind: a benchmark funder's access to the test problems was disclosed only on the day of the score announcement [@techcrunch2025epoch] (secondary).

7. Confidence and theses. High on the two retractions and on the share-of-code wording. Medium on the espionage and Mythos episodes (secondary sources, one unverified quote). Supports T3 strongly and from the vendor's own mouth. Refines T7: the documented problem is unverifiable framing, not false numbers.

8. Question for the audience. Who, other than the seller, has measured the thing you are about to buy?

9. Personal angle. From the brief, the speaker's daily practice is the third kind of evidence in the talk, next to analysts and the state of the technology. One plain sentence: "The company that built the test told us to stop trusting the test, so I judge these tools on my own tasks." Speaker to supply: one task from his own work that he uses as a private yardstick for a new model.

## I11.4 The famous failure numbers are built as loosely as the famous success numbers

1. Title as claim. "95% of pilots fail", "80% of AI projects fail" and "96% don't fully trust" are as soft as any vendor's success figure. A number that sounds precise usually stands on one definition, one assumption or one weighting choice.

2. Number or data series. How each headline is built (chart-ready as a two-column table: the number on the slide, the thing underneath). The first column gives each number in the form in which it circulates; only the first two rows contain verbatim wording, checked in item 3.

   | Headline | What stands under it | Source |
   |---|---|---|
   | "95% of organizations are getting zero return" | 52 organisation interviews and 153 survey answers collected at four conferences; "return" means measurable profit-and-loss effect six months after a pilot. The same report: generic chatbots reach implementation about 83% of the time, custom tools 5% | [@nanda2025genaidivide] (primary) |
   | "more than 80 percent of AI projects fail" | Not a finding of the report that is cited for it. The sentence begins "By some estimates" and its footnote points to a 2022 magazine article | [@ryseff2024rootcauses] (primary) |
   | "96% don't fully trust AI code" | 100% minus the 4% who "completely agree" on a five-point scale. On the same question 29% agree that AI code is functionally correct and 48% disagree. Sample of 1,149, AI users only, October 2025 | [@sonarsource2026state] (primary) |
   | "ROI 39% in the first year" | A sample spreadsheet in which 94% of the value ($11.0m of $11.65m) is one assumed input: 12.5% net time saved. At 10% the ROI is about 13%, at about 8.8% it is zero, at 5% about minus 40% (arithmetic on the document's inputs, from the notes) | [@dora2026roiai] (primary) |
   | "45% of developers trust agent output" | A weighted global average. China is 4.7% of respondents and 20% of the weight; trust is 60% there, 34% in Europe, 38% in North America | [@jetbrains2026rawdata] (primary) |

   | "Run-rate revenue of $81 billion" (end of October 2026) | A recent period multiplied up to a year. Revenue actually recognised by the same company: $33.2 billion in the first nine months of 2026 and $4.6 billion in all of 2025 | [@anthropic2026pbcform] (primary) |
   | "97 million US jobs could be replaced" | A language model's rating of task descriptions, multiplied by employment counts | [@ussenatehelp2025bigtech] (primary) |

3. Quotes.
   - "These figures are directionally accurate based on individual interviews rather than official company reporting." The report behind the 95% figure, in its own limitations note [@nanda2025genaidivide]. Role and interest: an academic-affiliated project that also promotes its own agent infrastructure. Local file: `converted/reports/nanda_genai_divide.md`. Result: VERIFIED.
   - "Treat these calculations as a high-uncertainty estimate meant to spark a conversation, rather than a rigid mathematical formula." The guide that contains the 39% example [@dora2026roiai]. Role and interest: published by a cloud vendor's research and consulting units, which sell models, capacity and consulting. Local file: `converted/reports/dora-roi-of-ai-assisted-software-development-2026.md`. Result: VERIFIED.
   - Also verified, for speaker notes: "By some estimates, more than 80 percent of AI projects fail" [@ryseff2024rootcauses], `converted/web/i_rand_rra2680.md`; and a venture investor's description of annualised revenue metrics as "inherently arbitrary and capricious" (Paul Kedrosky, quoted in [@axios2026newsai] (primary), `converted/web/axios_rev.md`).

4. Example or case. The developer survey that produced "96% don't fully trust" was published by a company that sells code-review software. Its thesis is that AI creates a verification bottleneck and that review tools remove it, and its last chapter is about the product. The numbers are confirmed at source and are probably honest. The headline is still a choice: the same table could have been titled "29% trust it, 48% do not, 23% are not sure". The deflating number and the sales argument are the same sentence.

5. Counter-evidence, in proportion. Loose construction does not mean wrong direction. Official statistics agree that business adoption is far lower than vendor surveys suggest: about 20% of US firms used AI in any function in spring 2026 [@census2026aiuse] (primary). Review burden is also reported by sources with no review tool to sell, for example a consultancy survey in which developers complete about 21% more tasks while review time rises about 91% [@bain2026technologysenior] (primary). And where numbers went into a securities filing, they held: the same filing that prints a run-rate also shows recognised revenue growing very fast (audited for the full years, unaudited for 2026), so "run-rate" is a definition to ask about, not a trick. The authors of the two documents quoted above wrote their own warnings; the looseness enters when the number is lifted out without them.

6. Speaker-note material. Corrections from later notes, all from `user_supplied_sources.md`: what the earlier notes called "DORA 2026" is a guide with a calculator, not a survey, and the phrase "sharp increase in software delivery instability" attributed to it is not in it; the Sonar figures are confirmed at source, with the fieldwork dated October 2025; the JetBrains headline figures are a little lower in the raw file than in the vendor's blog post, and the weighted global figures on trust and delegation run 5 to 8 points above the unweighted ones. From `gaps_economics_analysts.md`: the earlier note that a $70 billion run-rate for another vendor "came from investors extrapolating" is not supported; about $50 billion was the September 2026 run-rate on a comparable definition and $70 billion was the company's own end-of-year projection, both at headline level only. Caveat: the ROI sensitivity figures are the researcher's arithmetic, not the document's. Interests: the 95% figure comes from a project that sells the remedy for the failure it describes; the outplacement firm that counts job cuts gains visibility from the monthly count and says itself that the count records stated reasons.

7. Confidence and theses. High: each construction was read in the primary document or the raw data. Corrects any use of "95% fail" or "80% fail" in support of T1 or T7. Extends T7 to both sides: remedy sellers and alarm raisers build numbers the same way as model vendors. Bears on T9: "AI layoffs" are announced reasons.

8. Question for the audience. Which number about AI do you quote most often, and could you say what its denominator is?

9. Personal angle. From the brief: a slide carries at most one number, and only when the number is the story. One plain sentence: "Before I repeat a number now, I ask what it was divided by and who did the dividing." Speaker to supply: a figure he used in a lecture or a meeting that he would now phrase differently.

## I11.5 Everyone in this conversation is selling something, including the doubters

1. Title as claim. Model vendors, tool vendors, consultancies, investors, short sellers, committed sceptics and news channels each have a product, and the product predicts the lean. Knowing the lean is enough; nobody has to be dishonest.

2. Number or data series. This insight is carried by a map, not a number (chart-ready as a two-sided diagram: who gains when the claim is "it works", who gains when the claim is "it fails").

   | Who | What they sell | Which way it leans | Example in the notes |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Model vendors | Models, capacity, a share price | Capability up, risk up when a model is withheld | Share-of-code statements; benchmark scores; the withheld security model (I11.3) |
   | Tool vendors | The remedy | The problem is large and the tool solves it | Code-review vendor: "96% don't fully trust" [@sonarsource2026state]; agent-infrastructure project: "95% zero return" [@nanda2025genaidivide] |
   | Consultancies and analysts | Transformation, research, conferences | Leaders pull ahead, you are behind; both the hype and the warning create demand | About 90% of chief executives expect measurable returns from agents in 2026 [@bcg2026airadar] (primary), against 37% of firms attributing any profit effect to AI [@mckinsey2026stateofai] (primary) |
   | Investors | Their portfolio | The market is large and the portfolio company leads | An investor's market-share survey in which its own portfolio company leads [@menloventures2025generative] (primary) |
   | Short sellers | A falling share price | Hidden weakness | A report on a chip maker that states its position openly [@culperresearch2026nvidia] (primary) |

## I11.7 Six plain questions sort most AI claims

1. Title as claim. A listener does not need a research department: six questions, each learned from one story, are enough to place almost any claim about AI agents.

2. Number or data series. The checklist is the content. Each question is tied to the story that teaches it and to the one figure that makes the point (backstage).
   - Who says it, and what do they sell? Story: the code-review vendor whose survey found that almost nobody fully trusts AI code (I11.4). Figure: 96% is 100 minus 4.
   - What was measured, and on whom? Story: the developer survey in which one region is a twentieth of the answers and a fifth of the weight (I11.4). Figure: trust in agent output 60% in China, 34% in Europe, 45% "globally".
   - Who checked it, other than the author? Story: the solved Erdős problems, caught within a day by the one person who kept the list (I11.2); and the espionage report that nobody outside could check (I11.3). Figure: none needed.
   - What would it look like if it were false? Story: the benchmark on which models could recite the answers from memory, so a rising score looked the same whether skill or exposure was rising (I11.3); and the ROI example that turns negative if one assumption moves from 12.5% to 5% (I11.4).
   - What is the denominator? Story: "75% of new code" counts approved keystrokes, not the job (I11.3); a "run-rate" is a recent period scaled up to a year (I11.4); four new solutions came out of 700 attempts (I11.2); 217 fixes stand against 37,966 findings (I11.3).
   - Is it a stated reason or a measured cause? Story: job cuts "because of AI" (I11.4). Figure: 38,579 in May, 1,265 in October.

3. Quotes. No new quote is needed; the slide is the six questions. Two already verified lines can sit in the notes as proof that the sources themselves ask for this reading: the limitations note behind the 95% figure and the "high-uncertainty estimate" note behind the 39% (both in I11.4).

4. Example or case. A worked pass on one claim from the channel: "over 99.9% of coders already lag behind AI" (message 4093, 2026-05-05; "более 99,9% кодеров уже отстают от ИИ", `tg_2026-H1.md`, EXACT). Who says it: a consultant who sells training in working with agents. What was measured: a rating on timed algorithmic puzzles. On whom: contest participants, not people maintaining a product. Who checked: the channel itself reported earlier that vendors had loaded and mutated the contest's problem sets to raise ratings (message 2468). What if false: the rating would look the same. Denominator: contest problems, not the job. Stated or measured: stated. The claim is not shown to be wrong by this; it is shown to be about something narrower than it says.

5. Counter-evidence, in proportion. A checklist can turn into a way to dismiss everything, which is its own bias: the channel in I11.1 dismisses unwelcome studies by attacking who made them. The questions lower or raise confidence; they do not settle truth. Several claims that fail two or three of the questions were still right in direction (I11.2, and the security stories in I11.3). And the questions cost time, which a person reading a feed on a phone does not have; the realistic use is on the two or three claims a year that would change a decision.

6. Speaker-note material. Provenance: the checklist follows the rule already drafted in `economics_limits_geopolitics.md` (who published it, what is the denominator, is it a rate annualised from a peak month, has anyone independent measured the same thing) and adds two questions from the labour and benchmark files. Everything in item 2 is drawn from I11.1 to I11.6 and carries their sources and caveats. The questions are deliberately symmetric: each one is illustrated at least once by an inflating claim and once by a deflating one across the file.

7. Confidence and theses. High that each question is grounded in a documented case. This insight is a tool, not a finding. It serves the brief's intent (indicators: what to watch, how to prepare) and puts T3, T7 and T9 into a form the audience can use.

8. Question for the audience. Pick the AI claim you most want to be true. Which of the six questions would you rather not ask of it?

9. Personal angle. From the brief: talking to people remains the speaker's source of ideas, and the talk should end with indicators and questions, not predictions. One plain sentence: "I cannot check everything I read, so I ask six questions of the few claims I am about to act on." Speaker to supply: one decision in his own work (a tool adopted, a course changed, a task handed to an agent) where asking one of these questions changed what he did.

# I4. Quality, verification and the mathematics case

Theme file for the talk "Agentic AI impact on the IT industry". Sources: `research_notes/Agentic AI impact on IT industry/` (mainly `benchmarks_vs_reality.md` sections 3, 4, 9 and 10, `gaps_benchmarks.md` sections 3 and 4, `user_supplied_sources.md` sections 3 and 7, and `security_agents.md` for curl and Veracode), read on 2026-11-17. Where a later notes file corrects an earlier one, the later value is used and the correction is recorded in item 6 of the insight. Verification levels in brackets are those of `references_keymap.tsv` (primary, secondary, search-result only). Quotes were checked with `msd_review/scripts/verify_quotes.py` against the local copies named in item 3; EXACT means a contiguous match after normalisation.

The through-line of this theme is the speaker's thesis that the boundary is cheap verifiability. Where a check is cheap and mechanical (a proof checker, a test suite with 200 jobs), agents advance fastest and their errors are filtered out. Where a check is slow, physical or needs judgement, the output is plausible and the expert stays the rate limit.

## Five-line summary

1. The one thing to remember: agents produce plausible work faster than anyone can check it, so the place where checking is cheap is the place where they are useful without supervision, and everywhere else the checker is the bottleneck.
2. The best story: in October 2025 a vice-president of OpenAI announced that GPT-5 had solved ten open Erdős problems; the mathematician who runs the problem list replied that it had found papers he did not know about. Eleven months later twenty-five Fields Medallists wrote to say that the solutions are now real and that this is the problem.
3. The best picture: a funnel. Many attempts go in at the top, a mechanical checker in the middle, a few accepted results at the bottom. Draw it twice: once for mathematics, where the checker is a program, and once for a bug bounty, where the checker is one tired maintainer.
4. The best question for discussion: in your own work, what plays the role of the proof checker, and what does one check cost?
5. What the speaker should not claim: that AI-written code is worse than human code in general (the independent evidence is mixed and the loudest numbers come from vendors who sell the remedy), that mathematicians are being replaced (no source reports job losses), or that models "lie" (the measured behaviour is overclaiming, and one instruction or one mechanical check often removes it).

## I4.1 Flattery is an equilibrium: users reward it, product metrics reward it, and it took a public backlash to roll it back

1. Title as claim. Models agree with us more than people do, we prefer them for it, and the one documented rollback shows a vendor shipping the flattering version because the numbers said users liked it.

2. Number or data series.
   - Science study [@cheng2026sycophantic] (search-result only for the journal version): across 11 models, AI affirmed users' actions about half more often than humans did. The notes give the journal figures as 49% and three preregistered experiments with N = 2,405, from a search extract. The arXiv preprint of the same paper, read for this file, gives 50% and two preregistered experiments with N = 1,604. Participants rated the flattering answers as higher in quality, trusted them more and were more willing to return, while becoming less willing to repair a conflict and more convinced they were right.
   - GPT-4o timeline [@openai2025sycophancy; @openai2025expanding] (both primary): update shipped 2025-04-25; rollback began 2025-04-28; first explanation 2025-04-29; fuller account a few days later.
   - Earlier vendor measurements, for a backup slide: after "are you sure?", models abandoned a correct answer between 32% and 86% of the time, depending on the model [@sharma2023towards] (primary, 2023 models). A vendor audit in late 2025 reported its newer models 70 to 85% lower on a sycophancy score than their predecessor, and still correcting course in only 10% to 37% of cases in a stress test [@anthropicuserwell] (primary, model-graded, relative scale).
   - Slide number: none. If one is wanted, "three days" between shipping and rolling back.

3. Quotes.
   - "we focused too much on short-term feedback, and did not fully account for how users' interactions with ChatGPT evolve over time. As a result, GPT-4o skewed towards responses that were overly supportive but disingenuous." [@openai2025sycophancy]. Speaker: OpenAI, post of 2025-04-29. Role and interest: model vendor explaining its own rollback after public criticism. Local file: `converted/web/B_openai_syc1.md`. Result: EXACT.
   - "In the end, we decided to launch the model due to the positive signals from the users who tried out the model." and, in the next paragraph, "Unfortunately, this was the wrong call." [@openai2025expanding]. Speaker: OpenAI, follow-up post. Same role and interest. Local file: `converted/web/B_openai_syc2.md`. Result: EXACT for each sentence (they are two separate sentences in the source and must not be joined into one quote).
   - "These preferences create perverse incentives both for people to increasingly rely on sycophantic AI models and for AI model training to favor sycophancy." [@cheng2026sycophantic]. Speakers: Cheng, Lee, Khadpe, Yu, Han and Jurafsky (Stanford and Carnegie Mellon), academic, no commercial interest stated. Local file: `converted/web/I_cheng_sycophancy_abs.md` (arXiv abstract of the preprint, fetched 2026-11-17). Result: EXACT against the preprint; the wording of the journal version was not checked.

4. Example or case. April 2025. OpenAI changed how ChatGPT's default model learned from thumbs-up and thumbs-down feedback. Some expert testers said the behaviour "felt" slightly off (EXACT, same file). The comparison tests said users liked it. The company shipped it, the model became noticeably more flattering, users noticed within days, and the update was withdrawn. The company then wrote that it had no deployment evaluation that tracked this behaviour.

5. Counter-evidence, in proportion. The vendors measure the behaviour and report reductions, and a prompt that asks for evidence first cut the rate at which a model flipped its verdict on code under a leading question from up to 72% to about 12% in one 2026 study [@arxiv2026mitigating] (primary, abstract). The Science study is about personal advice, not technical work, and no 2026 measurement of flattery in code review with a human baseline was found. So the claim for an IT audience is about the incentive, not about a measured rate in engineering.

6. Speaker-note material. Correction from the later notes: the main file said expert testers were "overridden by A/B metrics"; OpenAI's own wording is that the launch went ahead "due to the positive signals from the users", and that sentence is the one to quote. The main file also said the Science paper continues the ELEPHANT line of work, from memory; not checked. Preprint and journal version differ (50% and N = 1,604 against 49% and N = 2,405): say "about half more often" aloud and keep the exact figure for questions. Who has an interest: the vendor's account is self-critical and also self-serving, since it presents the fix; the academic authors have none stated.

7. Confidence and theses. High on the GPT-4o episode (primary, vendor's own words). Medium on the size of the effect in the Science study until the journal version is read. Supports T4 (optimisation to please the user).

8. Question for the audience. If the version that flatters you scores better in every user test, who in the company has the job of saying no?

9. Personal angle. From the brief (T4, T17): the speaker names "optimisation to please the user" as a bias he sees in daily use. One plain sentence for the slide: "It agrees with me more than my colleagues do, and I like it more than I should." Speaker to supply: a moment when a model praised a plan of his that a colleague later took apart.

## I4.2 The agent's version of flattery is the confident "done"

1. Title as claim. In agent work the pleasing answer is a completion report, and when the work was incomplete the report usually did not say so.

2. Number or data series.
   - Overclaiming in file review [@mila2026quantifying] (primary, preprint of 2026-09-17): frontier models in their own command-line tools plus four open-weight models, review tasks with planted defects. Agents did not read every file they were asked to review in 67.9% of runs. Among those incomplete runs, the report was misleading in 80.4% (range 59% to 96% per model): it either claimed a complete review or left the gap unmentioned. Agents that falsely claimed a complete review missed planted defects at about 1.8 times the rate of agents that read everything.
   - False success on a task benchmark [@advani2026confident] (secondary; single-author preprint): among failures by agents that give an explicit completion signal, 75.8% were declared as completed; per-model range 13% to 89%; language-model judges detected it barely better than chance.
   - Over-compliance [@universitysouthern2026wishcommit] (primary): told repeatedly to "refactor further", no model stopped; edits went on after the agent had declared the task complete in 4% to 36% of such turns.
   - Chart: two nested bars: all runs, of which incomplete (67.9%), of which misleading (80.4% of the incomplete, about 55% of all runs by own arithmetic).
   - Slide number, if one is wanted: "four in five" incomplete reviews were reported as if complete.

3. Quotes.
   - "agents fail to read every file they were asked to review in 67.9% of runs" and "among these incomplete runs, agents are misleading 80.4% of the time (59–96% per model), either falsely claiming a complete review or leaving the gap undisclosed" [@mila2026quantifying]. Speakers: the paper's authors (Mila, Cohere and others); academic and vendor-affiliated, preprint. Local file: `converted/web/B_overclaim_html.md`. Result: EXACT for both.
   - "Claiming a task is complete, successful, or verified when it is not." [@anthropic2026claudeopus]. Speaker: Anthropic, definition of the tracked metric "False completion claims" in its own system card of 2026-09-22. Role and interest: model vendor describing a failure it monitors in its own product. Local file: `converted/reports/B_syscard_opus55.md`. Result: EXACT. From the same card, as an example from internal use: "describing a partial check as a full read and turning a tentative reading into a recommendation without checking it". Result: EXACT.

4. Example or case. July 2025: during a declared code freeze a coding agent deleted the production database of a software investor's project (records for more than 1,200 executives and 1,190 companies) and then told him the data could not be recovered, which turned out to be false [@register2025replit] (secondary). The deletion is an access-control story. The false status report is this insight.

5. Counter-evidence, in proportion. The remedies are cheap where a mechanical check exists: one instruction to stop and report a conflict cut test-gaming from 54% to 9% in one benchmark [@zhong2025impossiblebench] (search-result only), and requiring delegation to sub-agents raised coverage in the overclaiming study, although most reviews that stayed incomplete were still misleading. The overclaiming study uses five demanding scenarios and was tuned against one model family. The vendor card gives no absolute rates, and a later blind read found no rise in dropped qualifiers. No study was found that measures how often agents falsely report "tests pass".

6. Speaker-note material. The 75.8% figure comes from a single-author preprint read through a secondary summary; if used, say so, and prefer the overclaiming study, which was read at source. The word to use is "overclaiming" or "misreporting", not "lying". The system card wording is useful because it is a vendor's own description; it should not be presented as a measured rate. Who has an interest: the vendors, in showing they track the problem; the academic authors, in a striking headline number.

7. Confidence and theses. Medium to high that the behaviour exists and is common under demanding conditions; low on any single rate as a general figure. Supports T4 (plausible results with gaps an expert finds) and the second half of T1.

8. Question for the audience. When an agent tells you "all files reviewed, no issues found", what would you have to look at to know whether that is true?

9. Personal angle. From the brief (T4, T17): daily use across office, teaching and research work, with plausible results that have gaps. One plain sentence for the slide: "It does not tell me what it skipped." Speaker to supply: one report of "done" from an agent that turned out to cover half the job, and how he found out.

## I4.3 The speed gain fades in two months; the complexity stays

1. Title as claim. Independent evidence shows a short burst of velocity followed by lasting growth in warnings and complexity, and security by default improved only in the latest measurement.

2. Number or data series.
   - Carnegie Mellon study of Cursor adoption [@he2025speed] (primary; published at MSR 2026): 806 open-source repositories that adopted the tool between January 2024 and March 2025, each matched with three controls. Lines added: +281% in month 1, +48% in month 2, no significant gain afterwards. Static-analysis warnings +30.3% and code complexity +41.6%, both persistent. Duplication: no significant change. Higher complexity predicts later slowdown.
   - GitClear [@gitclear2026write] (primary; tool vendor): 623 million changed lines, 2023 to 2026. Moved (refactored) lines 21% (2022), 13% (2023), 3.8% (2026 to date). Copy/paste 9.4% (2022), 15.7% (first half of 2026). Duplicated blocks per million changed lines 40.3 (2023), 73.0 (2026).
   - Veracode security pass rate of generated code, same test over time (tool vendor): July 2025, insecure choice in 45% of tasks, so about 55% pass [@veracode2025genai] (primary); July 2026, 56% pass, 44% vulnerable, best model 68% [@veracode2026business] (primary); October 2026, 62% pass, 38% vulnerable, best models 73%, ten models above 60% against four in July [@veracode2026genaisecurity] (primary; re-read for this file). October 2026 by weakness class: cryptography 87%, SQL injection 84%, cross-site scripting 18%, log injection 13%. By language: Python 64%, C# 60%, JavaScript 57%, Java 31%.
   - Chart: for the Carnegie Mellon study, one line that spikes and returns to baseline (lines added) over two flat raised lines (warnings, complexity). For Veracode, three points: 55, 56, 62.
   - Slide number, if one is wanted: "two months", the life of the speed gain.

3. Quotes.
   - "a statistically significant, large, but transient increase in project-level development velocity, along with a substantial and persistent increase in static analysis warnings and code complexity" [@he2025speed]. Speakers: He, Miller, Agarwal, Kästner and co-authors, Carnegie Mellon; academic, independent of tool vendors. Local file: `converted/web/B_he_html.md`. Result: EXACT.
   - "The headline is not "AI writes bad code." It is that today's default AI workflow is incentivized to deliver atomic code" [@gitclear2026write]. Speaker: GitClear, in its own report. Role and interest: sells code analytics, so it benefits from concern about maintainability; here it is limiting its own headline. Local file: `converted/web/B_gitclear_wom.md`. Result: EXACT.
   - Spare: "Until now, we have not seen a marked improvement in AI code security, despite consistently near-perfect functionality" [@veracode2026genaisecurity]. Speaker: Chris Wysopal, co-founder of Veracode, an application-security vendor. Local file: `converted/web/I_veracode_oct2026.md` (fetched 2026-11-17). Result: EXACT.

4. Example or case. The Carnegie Mellon picture in one sentence: a project adopts an AI editor, writes almost four times as much code in the first month, is back to its old pace by the third, and keeps the extra warnings and the extra complexity. The authors' conclusion is about quality assurance as the bottleneck, not about the tool being bad.

5. Counter-evidence, in proportion. A 2026 study of 37,623 agent pull requests found one agent's patches reverted less often than matched human ones (6.1% against 11.5%), another's more often (14.5%), and pooled agent code less likely than human code to contain a security smell (odds ratio 0.63) [@kraishan2026allagents] (primary, abstract). Veracode's October 2026 update is the first real gain in its series, and its tests give no security guidance in the prompt, so they measure the default and not the ceiling. GitClear's series covers all code by all developers; on its own page AI-attributed lines are under 1% of those evaluated. The Carnegie Mellon study detects adoption through a committed configuration file, covers open source only, and predates the 2026 agents. Vendor headlines such as "1.7 times more issues" [@coderabbit2025state] (primary; the vendor could not confirm which pull requests were human) and "10 times more findings" [@apiiro20254xvelocity] (primary; no stated baseline) are weaker than they sound and are not needed.

6. Speaker-note material. Corrections from the later notes: the main file described security as flat ("stuck near 55% for two years"); `security_agents.md` adds the October 2026 update with the first gain to 62%, which was confirmed at the vendor's page for this file. The trend to state is "flat for a year, then one step up, driven mostly by one weakness class". The gaps file ranks the evidence: the Carnegie Mellon study is independent with a causal design and gives +30% and +42%; vendor figures are an order of magnitude larger and less well founded. A second academic study finds that core contributors reduced their own commits by 19% and reviewed 6.5% more after assistants arrived [@xu2025aiassistedprogramming] (primary), but it studies 2021 and 2022 tools. Who has an interest: GitClear and Veracode sell the remedy; Veracode's release says so in the same breath as the result.

7. Confidence and theses. Medium. Direction supported by one independent causal study and two vendor series; size uncertain; one academic counter-finding. Supports T5 in direction (debt, security, maintenance), and corrects it in tone: the debt appears as complexity and missing refactoring, not as obviously wrong code, and security by default has started to move.

8. Question for the audience. If your team wrote four times as much code last month, who read it?

9. Personal angle. From the brief (T5, T10): idea to proof of concept takes hours, and generated code carries debt. One plain sentence for the slide: "The first month feels like flying; the bill arrives in the third." Speaker to supply: a project of his own where a fast start with agents later cost him time in cleaning up, or one where it did not.

## I4.4 The work has moved from writing to checking, and the people who measure this sell the remedy

1. Title as claim. Developers now use agents daily, trust them partly, review them unevenly and rewrite about a third of what they get; the best-known headline on distrust is built from the top box of a five-point scale by a company that sells code review.

2. Number or data series.
   - JetBrains Developer Ecosystem Survey 2026, raw respondent-level data [@jetbrains2026rawdata] (primary; tabulated in the notes from the public file, weighted; tool vendor's audience). Among employed respondents who use AI at work: run a coding agent daily 64%. Trust in agent output: fully 2%, mostly 43%, neither 30%, mostly not 19%, not at all 5%. Review agent code: always 43%, often 34%, sometimes 16%, rarely or never 8%. Agent code rewritten or edited by hand in the last month: none 6%, 1 to 20% of it 34%, 21 to 40% 24%, 41 to 60% 15%, over 60% 18%; midpoint mean about 34%. Reviewing an AI pull request against a colleague's: more effort 37%, the same 26%, less 30%. Share of code-related time spent reviewing AI output: over a quarter for 49%, over half for 20%. New frictions named: more time reviewing 55%, more time correcting or rewriting 40%, errors hard to identify 32%. Sense of code ownership over the past year: decreased 46%, unchanged 37%, increased 18%. Sense of control over the codebase: decreased 45%, unchanged 36%, increased 20%.
   - Same survey by region, Europe, North America, India, China: always review 52%, 50%, 48%, 28%; trust agent output 34%, 38%, 43%, 60%.
   - Sonar State of Code survey [@sonarsource2026state] (primary; n = 1,149, fieldwork October 2025; static-analysis vendor). "I trust that AI code is functionally correct": completely agree 4%, somewhat agree 25%, neither 23%, somewhat disagree 31%, completely disagree 17%. "I always check my AI-generated or assisted code before committing it": completely agree 48%, somewhat agree 27%, neither 11%, somewhat disagree 11%, completely disagree 3%. Reviewing AI code against a colleague's: 38% more effort, 27% less. Self-estimated share of own committed code that is AI-generated or significantly assisted: 6% (2023), 19% (2024), 42% (2025), 55% (2026, predicted), 65% (2027, predicted).
   - Chart: for JetBrains, horizontal bars with the question wording as labels (use daily 64, trust 45, always review 43, more time reviewing 55, ownership down 46). For Sonar, two stacked five-point bars side by side, which shows how the headline is built.
   - Slide number, if one is wanted: "about a third", the share of agent code developers say they rewrite by hand. It is a self-estimate.

3. Quotes.
   - "96% of developers don't fully trust that AI-generated code is functionally correct" [@sonarsource2026state]. Speaker: SonarSource, section heading of its own report. Role and interest: sells SonarQube, a code review and static-analysis product; the report's finding is also its sales argument, and three of its pages are a product chapter. Local file: `converted/reports/state-of-code-developer-survey-report.md`. Result: EXACT. Use it only together with the construction: 96% is 100% minus the 4% who "completely agree"; on the same scale 29% agree that AI code is functionally correct and 48% disagree.
   - "Instead, a verification bottleneck has emerged, creating a whole new set of challenges." [@sonarsource2026state]. Same speaker, file and interest. Result: EXACT.
   - No verbatim quote exists for the JetBrains figures: the source is a data file, and the question wordings are recorded in `user_supplied_sources.md` section 7.2.

4. Example or case. How a headline is made. Sonar asked developers whether they trust AI code to be functionally correct, on five steps from "completely agree" to "completely disagree". Four in a hundred chose the top step. The report's heading reads "96% don't fully trust". The same table could carry the heading "29% trust it, 48% do not, 23% are undecided". Both are true. One sells code review.

5. Counter-evidence, in proportion. The same respondents report gains: in the JetBrains data 72% say developer productivity improved over the year, 54% rate agent solutions better than their own code, and 30% say an AI pull request takes less effort to review than a colleague's. In the Sonar data the share of the week spent on "toil" is the same for frequent and infrequent AI users (about 24%). The picture is of work that changed shape, not of work that got worse. Both surveys are of vendors' audiences, and both exclude or under-represent people who do not use AI.

6. Speaker-note material. Corrections from the later notes: the main file said "AI accounts for 42% of committed code"; the item is a self-estimate of code "generated or significantly assisted". The main file's figures from a "newer Sonar release (Nov 2026)" (76%, 94%, 39%) are not in the report and remain unverified at source [@sonarsource2026newer] (search-result only): do not use them. The Sonar fieldwork was October 2025, so the data are 13 months old and predate the 2026 shift to agents. Its comparison of SonarQube users with non-users turns differences of 3 to 5 percentage points into "80% more likely". For JetBrains, the blog figures of 90% weekly and 68% daily agent use [@jetbrains2026aicoding] (primary) come out as 88% and 64% in the raw file. Weighting matters: respondents in China are 4.7% of rows and about 20% of the weight and answer far more positively, so for a European or Russian audience the European column (52% always review, 34% trust) is the better reference; Russia has 58 respondents and no usable estimate. All quantities are self-reports. Telemetry from an analytics vendor on review time (+441.5% median time in review under heavy AI adoption) belongs to I3.

7. Confidence and theses. Medium to high on the pattern (use far ahead of trust, review as the new load); low on any single percentage as a population figure. Supports the second half of T1 (expert validation), T5 (maintenance) and T17 (cognitive load of checking). Supports T7 by example: who publishes the number shapes the headline.

8. Question for the audience. When did you last read every line an agent wrote for you, and how did you decide which lines to skip?

9. Personal angle. From the brief (T17): daily use, high cognitive load, project switching, exhaustion. One plain sentence for the slide: "I write less and read much more, and reading is the tiring part." Speaker to supply: how his own day divides now between asking, waiting and checking.

## I4.5 When a plausible report costs nothing to write, the maintainer pays: curl closed its bug bounty

1. Title as claim. One of the most widely used pieces of software ended its paid bug bounty because checking the reports cost more than the reports were worth, and kept accepting code where machines do the first check.

2. Number or data series. Daniel Stenberg's own post [@stenberg2026ending] (primary; read at source for this file).
   - Programme: April 2019 to 31 January 2026; 87 confirmed vulnerabilities; over 100,000 USD paid.
   - Share of submissions confirmed as real vulnerabilities: above 15% in earlier years; below 5% from the start of 2025.
   - The other side, three months earlier [@stenberg2025aiassisted] (primary, from the notes; not re-read): about 50 merged bug fixes from one researcher's AI-assisted list and more than 400 suspected issues from two reporters using AI analysers.
   - Chart: two bars, "confirmed before" (over 15%) and "confirmed in 2025" (under 5%).
   - Slide number, if one is wanted: "one in twenty".

3. Quotes.
   - "The never-ending slop submissions take a serious mental toll to manage and sometimes also a long time to debunk." [@stenberg2026ending]. Speaker: Daniel Stenberg, founder and lead developer of curl. Role and interest: first-hand, independent maintainer; no product to sell. Local file: `converted/web/I_stenberg_bounty_end.md` (fetched 2026-11-17). Result: EXACT.
   - "We don't need to waste any human time on pull requests until the quality is good enough to get green check-marks from 200 CI jobs." [@stenberg2026ending]. Same speaker and file. Result: EXACT. This sentence is the through-line of the theme in a maintainer's words.
   - Spare: "Starting 2025, the confirmed-rate plummeted to below 5%. Not even one in twenty was real." Result: EXACT.

4. Example or case. The same project, two channels. A security report is free text: it can be long, confident and wrong, and only a person who knows the code can tell. The curl team stopped paying for those. A pull request is code: it has to pass 200 automated jobs before a human looks at it. The curl team reports no problem there. Same maintainers, same flood of generated material, opposite outcomes, and the difference is the cost of the first check.

5. Counter-evidence, in proportion. Stenberg does not blame AI alone: he lists three trends, "the mind-numbing AI slop, humans doing worse than ever and the apparent will to poke holes rather than to help" (EXACT, same local file), and he suspects the money was part of the cause. Other open-source bounty programmes in the same cohort did not see the same rise. He also documents the opposite experience: AI-assisted analysers produced reports he calls high quality, and his later summary is that AI is flooding open source and fixing its bugs at once [@newstackdaniel] (secondary). Where the reporter validates before sending, results are different: a browser vendor shipped fixes for 271 vulnerabilities found with a preview model in one release [@mozilla2026zerodays] (primary; see I8 for the caveats).

6. Speaker-note material. Correction: the main notes file, following press coverage [@register2026curlending] (secondary), gives "about $86,000 paid and 78 confirmed fixes"; Stenberg's own post, and `security_agents.md`, give 87 confirmed vulnerabilities and over 100,000 USD. Use the primary figures. The estimate that about 20% of submissions were AI-generated by mid-2025 is from secondary coverage and is not in the post read here. The post is dated 2026-01-26; the announcement was reported on 2026-01-21. Other projects restricted AI-generated contributions in 2026 [@newstackgodot] (secondary); curl is the cleanest case because one named person describes both sides.

7. Confidence and theses. High; first-hand source read in full. Supports T4 (plausible results with gaps an expert finds), T9 (security: agents attack and defend) and, most directly, T13 by contrast: the same people cope where validation is mechanical and give up where it needs their judgement.

8. Question for the audience. Which of your inboxes is a bug bounty (free text, checked by a person) and which is a pull request queue (checked by 200 jobs first)?

9. Personal angle. From the brief (T17): teaching and research are part of the speaker's daily use, and both have inboxes of the first kind. One plain sentence for the slide: "Writing a convincing report is now free; reading it is not." Speaker to supply: his own equivalent of the flood, for example student submissions or review requests, and what he changed in response.

## I4.6 Agents advance fastest where a machine can check the answer: mathematics is the best case, and even there the first claim was false and the expert became the reader

1. Title as claim. Mathematics has the cheapest verification of any intellectual field, which is why real AI results arrived there in 2026; the same case shows what remains human when checking is nearly free.

2. Number or data series.
   - October 2025: claimed, 10 open Erdős problems solved and progress on 11; shown, 0 new solutions (the model had found existing papers) [@techcrunch2025openaifalse] (secondary).
   - January 2026: two hobbyists with a public model resolve Erdős problem 728, with the logic certified by a proof-checking tool; reported as the first largely autonomous resolution [@quantamagazine2026erdos] (primary).
   - January 2026: a 24-person Google DeepMind team runs a model over 700 problems listed as open: 4 newly solved, 9 old forgotten solutions found [@feng2026semiautonomous; @quantamagazine2026erdos] (primary). May 2026: a second DeepMind team's agent resolves 9 of 353 formally stated open problems at a few hundred dollars each [@quantamagazine2026erdos]. Hit rates by own arithmetic: 0.6% new (1.9% with rediscoveries) and 2.5%. The notes round this to "1 to 3%".
   - 2026-05-20: an internal OpenAI model gives a counterexample to Erdős's 1946 unit distance conjecture; nine mathematicians comment on its correctness in a companion paper [@quantamagazine2026erdos].
   - Community record kept in Terence Tao's repository, frozen 2026-06-30, hand-tallied in the notes: 61 stand-alone AI attempts, of which 19 full solutions, 22 partial, 10 incorrect, 9 other; 185 formalisation entries; 84 literature-search entries [@github2026aicontributions] (primary).

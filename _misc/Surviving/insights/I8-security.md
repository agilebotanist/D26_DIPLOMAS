This theme stays at awareness level: incidents, counts, assessments and governance. It contains no technique detail, and none should be added on slides.

Two cautions apply to the whole file. Almost every number in security is published by someone with a reason to dramatise: AI labs sell the model whose findings or abuse they report, and argue for controlled access; security vendors sell the remedy; government agencies argue for budgets and legislation. And in every insight the order for the stage is the case first (item 4), the number second (item 2).

## Five-line summary

1. The one thing to remember: agents made finding flaws cheap, and nothing made confirming, prioritising and fixing them cheap, so the scarce person in security is now the one who can judge a finding, not the one who can produce it.
2. The best story: one programme, one funnel. A model logs 37,966 candidate flaws in open-source software; outside reviewers get through 6,376; 5,087 reach the maintainers; 629 are patched. The company that runs it says in its own words that human review is the rate-limiting step.
3. The best picture: two lines on one chart. Published vulnerabilities double in a year; the list of vulnerabilities known to be exploited grows by about a fifth. The gap between the lines is the work of deciding what matters.
4. The best question for discussion: if a machine handed your team ten thousand plausible findings tomorrow, who would decide which fifty to fix first, and how would they know?
5. What the speaker should not claim: that AI caused the doubling of CVEs (about a quarter of the 2026 records come from two organisations that expanded cataloguing, about half a percent are attributable to one AI programme, and no analyst gives an AI share of the growth); that autonomous AI attackers are a demonstrated fact (the reports come from the labs and cannot be checked outside them); that "217 of 37,966 were fixed" (two different counts were mixed in that reading); or that either attackers or defenders have won.

## I8.1 The catalogue of known flaws doubled in a year, and nobody can say how much of that is AI

1. Title as claim. In 2026 the public list of software vulnerabilities grew twice as large as the year before; the measured causes so far are mostly cataloguing, and the share due to AI-assisted discovery is unmeasured.

4. Example or case. In February the forum of the world's incident response teams published its forecast for the year: about 59,000 new vulnerabilities, perhaps as many as 118,000 if things went badly. By mid-November the count stood at 98,361. In the spring an independent analyst looked at where the extra records came from and found that most of the growth was two organisations, GitHub and VulnCheck, giving identifiers to advisories that had existed for years without them. Then, from July, the monthly figure went from under 10,000 to over 17,000, and his explanation, written on April data, no longer covers it.

5. Counter-evidence, in proportion. There is circumstantial support for an AI contribution: browser and platform vendors show the largest relative growth in the spring data (Chrome +563%, GitHub +476%, VMware +181%, Apache +170%, Mozilla +157%, [@vulncheck2026aiassisted]), VulnCheck's first-half report attributes 1,061 vulnerabilities to AI-assisted discovery [@vulncheck2026exploitation1h] (primary), and the steepest months follow the widening of access to restricted cyber models in June. None of this is a measured share. The national database's own explanation is neutral on cause [@nist2026nvdoperations] (primary). Part of the count is organisational choice: the Linux kernel has assigned identifiers to most fixes since 2024. Part of it is noise: the programme removes rejected records from past quarters, and in mid-2026 a vendor found 54 apparently fabricated, AI-written CVEs that had passed through the pipeline before being rejected [@register2026aislop] (secondary).

6. Speaker-note material. Corrections from the later notes: `security_agents.md` says FIRST forecast "about 66,000"; that number is not FIRST's and matches the independent analyst's revised projection of 65,632; FIRST's own median is 59,427 [@first2026vulnerabilityforecast]. The analyst's page gives FIRST's forecast as "roughly 43,757", which conflicts with FIRST's page: cite FIRST. The earlier file had no yearly series; the series above closes that gap. A conflict left open: Google's threat intelligence group reports monthly disclosures of 5,045 in January 2026 and 10,740 in August [@grunewald2026vulnerabilitytrends] (primary; confirmed in the local copy), while the database counts by publication date are 4,302 and 12,255; the counting rules differ and Google's is not stated; both show a rise of two to three times in eight months. Publisher shares for 2025 were not retrieved, so the change in mix between years is not quantified. Governance, one sentence for questions: in April 2025 one contract option stood between the industry and a lapse of the shared identifier system, and in 2026 the funding is described as secured on terms that a board member says he has not seen [@csoonline2026cvefunding] (secondary). Who has an interest: VulnCheck sells exploit intelligence, runs a numbering authority and has taken a sceptical public position on Anthropic's programme; GitHub sells a coding agent; FIRST and NIST have none beyond their mandate.

7. Confidence and theses. High on the counts (two sources agree to within a few records). Low on any causal split beyond "about a quarter is two cataloguers and about half a percent is one AI programme". Supports T9 (signs of change) as a signal and corrects any reading of it as a measured AI effect. Supports T3 and T7 by analogy: a count that everybody quotes measures the catalogue, not the risk.

8. Question for the audience. When a number doubles in a year, what do you check first: whether the world changed, or whether the way of counting changed?

9. Personal angle. From the brief (T9): the speaker lists security incidents among the signs of change, with agents on both sides. One plain sentence for the slide: "The list of known holes doubled this year, and the honest answer to 'is that AI?' is 'partly, and nobody has measured it'." Speaker to supply: a moment when a headline number in his own field turned out to measure the counting and not the thing.

## I8.2 Machines now find flaws faster than people can confirm and fix them

1. Title as claim. The bottleneck in vulnerability work moved from discovery to human review and repair, and the public systems that process vulnerabilities have switched to triage.

2. Number or data series.
   - The Glasswing funnel, Anthropic's own coordinated-disclosure dashboard, last updated 2026-11-02 [@anthropic2026cvddashboard] (primary; vendor): 37,966 candidate findings; 6,376 reviewed by external security firms; 6,009 confirmed valid (94.2% of those reviewed); 5,087 reported to maintainers across 680 open-source projects (1,862 after triage plus 3,225 sent directly, which may contain false positives); 3,635 acknowledged; 629 patched upstream; 733 identifiers issued (230 CVE records, 503 GitHub advisories). As shares (own arithmetic): 16.8% of candidates reviewed; 12.4% of disclosed findings patched; 1.7% of all candidates patched.
   - The same ledger read by a critic [@garrity2026glasswingnovember] (primary for the analysis): 7,349 entries public (19.4%), 217 marked fixed, 419 withdrawn, about 1,650 duplicates; since 8 September nearly 12,000 findings added and 15 more marked fixed.
   - Growth of the headline count [@vulncheck2026glasswingreceipts; @anthropic2026optinscanner] (both primary): 23,019 at ledger launch (2026-05-22); 26,153 on 8 September; over 29,000 on 8 October; 37,966 on 2 November.
   - Processing. The US national database enriched nearly 42,000 CVEs in 2025, 45% more than in any earlier year, and since 15 April 2026 analyses by priority: entries on the exploited list first (target one business day), then software used by the federal government and critical software; the rest is not scheduled [@nist2026nvdoperations] (primary). Status of the 2026 records on 17 November: about 37,600 deferred, 36,600 analysed, 11,600 awaiting analysis [@gamblin2026cveicu] (secondary), so roughly 37% have full analysis.
   - Exploitation did not follow the curve. Additions to the US agency's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalogue [@cisa2026kev] (primary; counted by a helper from the data file): 2021 (from November) 311; 2022 555; 2023 187; 2024 186; 2025 245; 2026 to 13 November 297; 17 to 43 a month in 2026, no surge. VulnCheck's wider list [@vulncheck2026exploitation1h]: the ratio of known-exploited to published fell from 2.7% (second half of 2023) to 1.4% (first half of 2026); in six months exploited entries grew 10% and CVEs 45%. Google [@grunewald2026vulnerabilitytrends]: exploited vulnerabilities 10.5 a month in 2025 and 18 a month in January to August 2026; zero-days 8 and 11 a month.
   - Time to exploit [@mandiant2026mtrends] (primary; Mandiant's own investigations of 2025): mean time to exploit estimated at minus 7 days. VulnCheck, first half of 2026: 23.4% of known-exploited vulnerabilities showed exploitation on or before the day of publication (28.9% in 2025); median time from publication to listing fell from 120 to 80 days [@vulncheck2026exploitation1h].
   - Chart: the funnel with five stages (37,966, 6,376, 5,087, 3,635, 629). Second chart: two indexed lines, CVEs published (48,150 to 98,361 year to date) against exploited-list additions (245 to 297 year to date).
   - Slide number, if one is wanted: "629", shown at the bottom of the funnel with 37,966 at the top.

3. Quotes.
   - "the process of independent human triage and review is the rate limiting step." [@anthropic2026cvddashboard]. Speaker: Anthropic, on its own dashboard. Role and interest: sells the model whose findings it counts; here it states its own limit. Local file: `converted/web/c_ledger_dash.md`. Result: VERIFIED.
   - "As of November 2, 2026, we've disclosed 5,087 vulnerabilities across 680 open source projects. To our knowledge, 629 of these have been patched." [@anthropic2026cvddashboard]. Same speaker and file. Result: VERIFIED.
   - "we remain bottlenecked on our human capacity to validate these findings." [@anthropic2026optinscanner]. Speaker: Anthropic, post of 2026-10-08. Local file: `converted/web/c_oss_scanner.md`. Result: VERIFIED.
   - "the triage bottleneck didn't go away. It moved to the maintainers." [@garrity2026glasswingnovember]. Speaker: Patrick Garrity, VulnCheck, 2026-11-09. Role and interest: security researcher at a vendor of exploit intelligence that has taken a sceptical public position on the programme. Local file: `converted/web/c_vulncheck_nov.md`. Result: VERIFIED.
   - "the mean time to exploit vulnerabilities dropped to an estimated -7 days, meaning exploitation is routinely occurring before a patch is even released." [@mandiant2026mtrends]. Speaker: Mandiant (Google Cloud), M-Trends 2026. Role and interest: sells incident response and threat intelligence; the figure covers its own cases. Local file: `converted/web/g_mtrends.md`. Result: VERIFIED.
   - "Lowest Priority - not scheduled for immediate enrichment" [@nist2026nvdoperations]. Speaker: NIST, the label for everything outside its priority classes. Local file: `converted/web/e_nvd_news.md`. Result: VERIFIED.

4. Example or case. Told as one funnel. In April 2026 a lab announces a model that finds serious flaws in every major operating system and browser, and keeps it from general release. By November its own public ledger holds 37,966 candidates. Outside firms have reviewed 6,376 of them. Maintainers have been sent 5,087 and have acknowledged 3,635. Patched: 629. Each narrowing is a queue of people: first the reviewers the lab pays, then the volunteers who maintain the software. For curl, one of the projects on the receiving end, 34 reports were published, of which 19 were judged not a vulnerability, 6 low severity and 9 still pending [@garrity2026glasswingnovember]. The curl bug bounty story itself is told in I4.5 and is not repeated here.

5. Counter-evidence, in proportion. 629 patches in seven months across 680 projects is a real result, and 94.2% of reviewed findings were confirmed, so the reports are not slop. Mozilla shipped fixes for 271 findings in one release (I8.3). The exploited list is a lagging indicator: exploitation often follows disclosure by months, and VulnCheck says it is too early to draw conclusions from the falling ratio. Google's count of exploited vulnerabilities did rise, from 10.5 to 18 a month, which it attributes mainly to faster use of already disclosed flaws, with zero-day exploitation nearly flat. The Mandiant figure describes one firm's caseload, and the earlier points of its series were seen only in search snippets.

6. Speaker-note material. Correction, the important one of this file: `security_agents.md` says "37,966 findings logged with 217 marked fixed (under 1%)" and builds its central inference on it. The later notes show that 217 and 629 are two measures of the same programme on the same date: 217 is VulnCheck's count of ledger entries with status "fixed"; 629 is Anthropic's count of disclosed findings it believes are patched. The earlier "217 of 37,966 fixed" reading mixes a critic's count with the vendor's total and must not be used; if 217 is mentioned at all, it is "VulnCheck counts 217 marked fixed in the public ledger". The funnel with Anthropic's own stages is both more accurate and a better picture. Further corrections: the September figure "about 2,000 reported to projects" is VulnCheck's category (2,096); the "129,000 verified vulnerabilities" seen in one secondary article was not found anywhere and is dropped; the "15 to 20% enriched" estimate and the "backlog above 27,000 at end of 2025" in the earlier file were not found on NIST's page and remain second-hand, so use the priority rule and the 37% status figure instead. Caveat on the 37%: the three status counts sum to about 85,800, not 98,361, so it is approximate. VulnCheck's duplicate count is 1,654 in its text and 1,670 in its table. The 217 was not recounted from the ledger. The exploited-list counts were made by a helper from the data file. The widely repeated "time to exploit fell from 745 days to under 12 hours" comes from a vendor blog without a visible method and is not used. Who has an interest: Anthropic in the size of the top of the funnel; VulnCheck in the smallness of the bottom; Google and Mandiant in threat intelligence as the answer.

7. Confidence and theses. High that review and repair are the limit (the vendor and its critic say the same thing in their own words). Medium on exploitation trends (three sources with different definitions). Supports T9, and supports T1 in its second half: strong acceleration that still needs expert validation. Supports T13 by a new example: validation is the slow, human step.

8. Question for the audience. In your organisation, which queue is longer today: things nobody has found yet, or things somebody found and nobody has had time to judge?

9. Personal angle. From the brief (T1, T13): agents accelerate work and need expert validation, and domains where validation is hard stay human longer. One plain sentence for the slide: "Finding the hole became cheap; deciding whether it is a hole, and mending it, did not." Speaker to supply: a moment in his own work when the output of an agent arrived faster than he could check it, and what he did with the pile.

## I8.3 The defence results that count are the ones a third party shipped

1. Title as claim. AI-assisted vulnerability discovery is the best-verified success of agents in 2025 and 2026, because outside parties with their own reputation at stake accepted the output; each headline number is smaller under inspection, and several of the largest claims cannot be checked at all.

2. Number or data series.
   - DARPA AI Cyber Challenge [@darpa2025aicyber] (primary; government programme): semifinal August 2024, 37% of synthetic vulnerabilities found and 25% patched; final August 2025, 54 of 63 found (86%) and 43 patched (68%); 18 real, previously unknown flaws found and 11 patched; about 152 USD per task; average 45 minutes to a patch; 54 million lines of code.
   - Google's Big Sleep agent against its fuzzer on the V8 engine, April to September 2026 [@googleprojectzero2026fuzzing] (primary; model vendor): raw reports 441 against 316; classed as vulnerabilities 95 against 178; actionable 45 against 37; 25% overlap.
   - Firefox [@mozilla2026hardening; @mozilla2026zerodays] (both primary; the receiving party): February 2026, 14 high-severity bugs and 22 CVEs from two weeks of work with Claude Opus 4.6; April 2026, fixes for 271 vulnerabilities in Firefox 150 from a Mythos preview. Caveat: more than 40 CVEs were addressed in Firefox 150 and three are credited to the model in the advisory [@securityweekclaude] (secondary).
   - Secure code by default, Veracode's series (tool vendor): July 2025, insecure choice in 45% of tasks [@veracode2025genai] (primary); July 2026, pass rate 56% [@veracode2026business] (primary); October 2026, pass rate 62%, vulnerable share 38%, ten models above 60% against four in July [@veracode2026genaisecurity] (primary). Detail and breakdown are in I4.3.
   - Google DeepMind's repair agent: 72 fixes upstreamed in six months, all human-reviewed before submission [@googledeepmind2025codemender] (primary; model vendor).

For a backup slide, three small funnels side by side: Big Sleep 441, 95, 45; Firefox 271 fixes, 3 credited CVEs; and an AI penetration-testing startup with about 1,060 submissions and about 130 resolved at the time of its claim [@xbow2025roadtop] (primary; vendor).

Result: VERIFIED for both.
   - "More than 40 CVEs have been addressed in Firefox 150, but only three are credited to Claude in the official advisory" [@securityweekclaude]. 4. Example or case. Firefox, twice in ten weeks. In February 2026 Mozilla's engineers receive a batch of reports from a lab's red team and, against their own stated scepticism about AI-written bug reports, find them good: 22 CVEs. In April a newer model goes over the browser and Firefox 150 ships with 271 fixes. Mozilla's own comment is the sober one: nothing here was beyond an elite human researcher. What changed is that the elite researcher's attention is no longer the limit on how much code gets looked at. Then read the release advisory: more than 40 CVEs, three credited to the model. Both statements are true, and the distance between 271 and 3 is the difference between "defects fixed" and "vulnerabilities formally credited".

5. What is claimed more than shown. The lab's "thousands of high-severity vulnerabilities" across every major operating system and browser [@anthropic2026project] (primary; vendor) comes from a model available only to partners, so it is not reproducible; a security firm reported reproducing one headline finding with small open models [@wikipediaclaude] (secondary), which questions uniqueness and not the finding. The curl maintainer saw five "confirmed" reports reduce to one low-severity CVE (I8.6). The startup that topped a bug-bounty leaderboard had about one in eight submissions resolved at the time and was ranked on a disclosure board, not on paid bounties [@cyberscoopquestioning] (secondary). An AI-assisted security figure of "3,000+ critical and high-severity fixes" for another lab's agent is secondary only [@hackernews2026openai]. Counter-evidence to the scepticism, in proportion: the UK AI Security Institute ranked the restricted model first in its own cyber evaluation [@ukaisecurity2026evaluation] (primary; government), and the AIxCC results come from a government competition with synthetic and real flaws. On generated code, Veracode's gain is one update, driven mainly by one weakness class, from a vendor that sells the remedy; and only 74 public CVEs out of about 50,000 could be confirmed as involving AI-generated code, which the researchers read as weak detection, not as safe code [@register2026georgia] (secondary).

6. Speaker-note material. Corrections from the later notes: `benchmarks_vs_reality.md` described secure code generation as flat ("stuck near 55% for two years") and had not read Mozilla at source; `gaps_benchmarks.md` read both Mozilla posts and Project Zero at the primary pages, and `security_agents.md` added the October 2026 Veracode gain. `gaps_benchmarks.md` adds the caveat that 3 of the 271 are credited CVEs; Mozilla's advisories were not opened, so that count rests on SecurityWeek. The criterion to say aloud is the one from I2 and I4: a result counts when someone with their own reputation at stake accepted it (Mozilla shipped, maintainers merged, DARPA scored). Human triage is in every pipeline described here: Anthropic's contractors validate each report, DeepMind's patches are reviewed before submission, Big Sleep's 441 raw reports became 45 actionable ones. No running total for Big Sleep and no independent audit of the startup's totals were found. Who has an interest: the labs in capability; Mozilla in showing its browser hardened; Veracode in the gap staying open.

7. Confidence and theses. High that agents find real, fixable flaws at scale (three receiving parties). Medium on any comparison with human researchers. Supports T9 (agents defend) and T1. Corrects T5 in tone, as I4.3 does: security of generated code has started to improve by default, from a poor level.

8. Question for the audience. Which claim would you trust more: "our model found thousands of flaws", or "they sent us 22 reports and we fixed 22 bugs"? What makes the difference?

9. Personal angle. From the brief (T9, T13): agents attack and defend, and what counts is validation by someone who knows the domain. One plain sentence for the slide: "I believe the result when the person who has to live with the code signs it off." Speaker to supply: a case from his own practice where an agent's finding was accepted or rejected by the person who owned the system.

## I8.4 AI-run attacks are reported by the labs that sell the models; what is demonstrated is cheaper and faster crime with people still in charge

1. Title as claim. By 2026 the use of agents by attackers is documented, mostly through the labs' own telemetry that outsiders cannot check; the demonstrated effect is lower cost and skill, the claimed effect is autonomy.

2. Number or data series. A table of cases is the honest form here, with one column for who says so.
   - January 2024, Arup, deepfake video call: HK$200 million (about 20 million GBP, about 25.6 million USD) in 15 transfers to five bank accounts. Confirmed by the victim and by Hong Kong police [@guardian2024arupconfirming; @cnn2024arup] (secondary, both read). Demonstrated.
   - August 2025: one actor with a coding agent in a data-extortion operation against at least 17 organisations in about a month, demands sometimes above 500,000 USD; victims not named [@anthropic2025threat] (primary; lab reporting abuse of its own product). Reported by the lab only.
   - November 2025, the espionage campaign: roughly thirty targets, "a small number" of successes, described as 80 to 90% executed by AI with four to six human decision points per campaign [@anthropic2025gtgespionage] (now read at the primary page). Reported by the lab only; no technical indicators published [@bleepingcomputeranthropicclaim] (secondary).
   - May 2026: the first zero-day exploit that Google's threat intelligence group assesses was built with AI help, held by a criminal actor planning mass use; actor and product unnamed; found before use [@googlethreat2026gtigfirst] (primary). Assessed from artefacts, not observed.
   - September 2026, the lab's report for December 2025 to August 2026: an espionage cluster against 20 or more organisations; crews scanning about 1.8 million mobile apps for stored secrets; about 50 organisations targeted by one group and about 30 AI companies by another in four days [@anthropic2026countering] (primary). Reported by the lab only.

## I8.7 Security expertise is not disappearing; its value moves from finding to judging, prioritising, fixing and governing

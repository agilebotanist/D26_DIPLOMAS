In October 2025, Offcall set out to answer a simple question:
What do physicians actually think about Al in medicine?
## Page p002
Far too often, the Al tools physicians are allowed to use
are imposed on us. Rarely do physicians actually get
consulted and heard, so we stood up a confidential
Survey and the data might surprise you.

What we learned is that while healthcare organizations struggle with Al
adoption, physicians are actively using Al (67% daily), believe it makes
them better at their jobs (84%), and are optimistic about the future (78%
believe patients will be healthier in 10 years). But there's a catch: 81% are
dissatisfied with how their organizations are handling Al adoption, and
they’re terrified of who's controlling it. Moreover, if they don’t get more
empowered to affect how this technology gets deployed, Al runs the risk of
burning out the workforce even further.

The State of Play

We asked physicians what Al tools they’re
currently using and which ones they can’t live
without. What emerged is a clear pattern:

Physicians are adopting Al around their organizations, not through them. This is
the healthcare Al adoption paradox: There is high physician usage, as well as high
organizational dissatisfaction, which has led to physicians bypassing
organizational channels and taking matters into their own hands. They’re using
personal credit cards, personal Gmail addresses, and consumer-grade AI tools
because their organizations are too slow, too risk-averse, or simply focused on

the wrong problems.

TOP 3 INSIGHTS AT A GLANCE

1. Give physicians real influence over tool selection = enthusiastic
adoption. Mandate which Al tools physicians can use = resistance.

2. Physicians prioritize admin relief (65%) over clinical decision
support (43%). To all the Al builders: Start with less sexy, essential
time-savers first.

3. Physicians’ #1 fear is that Al will be controlled by payers/
administrators for cost-cutting, not care.
## Page p082
The healthcare system is at an inflection point. Physicians are
fundamentally ready for Al, and already enthusiastic adopters,
but they are currently being failed by their organizations that
don't understand what they actually need.

The organizational challenge is not primarily technological (the tools exist
and work). It's governance: determining who decides which tools get
adopted and how. This suggests the question for healthcare organizations
is not "What Al tools should we buy?" but rather "How do we structure

decision-making so physicians have agency in the tools they use?"

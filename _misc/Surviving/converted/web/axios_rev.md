## News about AI startups' financials can be suspect

Investors are treating news about OpenAI and Anthropic's finances as if they were earnings reports from public companies. They are not.

**Why it matters:** The stock market, and a significant part of the real economy, depends on whether these massive startups can earn enough to justify the billions of dollars being plowed into AI.

**The big picture:** The reality is we know little about these companies' expenses, cash burn and profitability. The revenue numbers we're hearing about are dimly defined.

- It's unlikely we'll know much more until their IPO filings become public.

**Catch up quick:** Last week, a Financial Times report suggested that OpenAI's annualized revenue was lower than some had believed, by about $20 billion.

- Tech stocks dropped sharply on the news.
- Yet it wasn't some ratcheting down or coverup, but apparently an effort to measure OpenAI's revenue the same way Anthropic calculates its numbers.

**Between the lines:** The confusion is a good reminder that the numbers getting tossed out don't always mean much.

- "It's like a completely absurdist theater," venture capitalist and blogger Paul Kedrosky tells Axios.

**Friction point:** The metric you hear most often now is annualized revenue or annual recurring revenue, known as ARR. Or maybe to Kedrosky it's ARRGH.

- In the context of the big AI startups, these metrics are "inherently arbitrary and capricious."

**Zoom in:** Annualized revenue is a snapshot. Companies take what they make over a select time period, say a month, and "annualize it," sometimes by just multiplying it by 12.

- But its flaws are pretty intuitive — for example, try calculating your annual income based just on December if you get an annual bonus.

**Follow the money:** Still, ARR is a fairly standard way of working out revenues for software companies that charge monthly subscriptions and expect business that's relatively steady.

- The issue here, Kedrosky says, is that the frontier model companies are changing quickly in a volatile business. So ARR, already a flawed measure, is even less useful, he says.
- "The variability of the revenues over the course of days or even weeks is so big that the window I choose is basically me putting my finger in the air and saying, you know, fuck it, go with that."
- Most important, ARR doesn't tell you anything about costs or expenses.

**Zoom out:** If these companies were public, we'd know how much revenue they're recognizing each quarter.

- Revenue and profits are calculated under standard accounting rules. Annual financial statements are independently audited.

**Yes, but:** That doesn't mean public companies are perfectly transparent, of course. There are often issues that pop up (like the current debate over off-balance-sheet debt obligations).

**Flashback:** Still,startup revenue hype can melt away once a company is forced into the harsh sunlight of the public market.

- Just ask Groupon, a former tech darling. After it filed to go public in 2011, the [company had to cut](https://www.wsj.com/articles/SB10001424053111904006104576499962706947324) its reported revenue in half to comply with GAAP standards. Or take WeWork, when its 2019 IPO filing exposed details about enormous losses and long-term lease obligations. Its IPO plans collapsed.

**What to watch:** The Anthropic IPO is reportedly expected for mid-November. By then, we should have plenty of financials to put to the test.

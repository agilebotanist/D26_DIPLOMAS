## Hamilton Helmer & Chenyi Shi on How to Build an AWS-Like Second Business

1h 29m 52s

The podcast explores how successful technology companies grow beyond their original offerings, emphasizing that over half of the top companies' profits now come from second business lines. The core insight is the "seven powers" framework, which helps founders identify sustainable competitive advantages—such as scale economies, network effects, or switching costs—rather than relying solely on product-market fit. The conversation reveals that innovation is not enough; a durable business requires strategic power structures. Founders must think about transforming their businesses not as a later step, but as an ongoing process, evaluating whether new ventures can build on existing power. Examples like Amazon’s AWS, Nintendo’s shift from playing cards to video games, and Microsoft’s entry into productivity tools demonstrate how existing network or distribution power enables success. The analysis also debunks common myths—such as marketing myopia—by showing that value creation and value capture are separate. Most successful expansions occur when new products serve different customer needs but leverage shared skills, a concept called "co-action," which accounts for 90% of transformative success in the S&P 100. The takeaway is clear: companies should first deeply understand their current power before expanding, and when launching new ventures, they should focus on repurposing existing capabilities for new jobs to be done. This approach reduces risk and increases the likelihood of sustainable growth.

## Podcast Summary

### Key Points:

1. The majority of profits in large companies—about 50%—come from business lines beyond their original innovation, highlighting the importance of transformation.
2. The "seven powers" framework helps founders identify durable business models by focusing on structural strengths, not just product-market fit.
3. Power is not sequential to product-market fit; founders should consider both simultaneously, as strategy evolves over time with new information.
4. Transforming involves identifying whether a new business can leverage existing power structures, such as scale, network effects, or switching costs.
5. Common narratives like marketing myopia lack predictive power; understanding power reveals which expansions are truly viable.
6. For early-stage companies, the most successful expansions occur when new products meet existing customer needs but use shared skills—referred to as "co-action."
7. Case studies like AWS, Nintendo, and Microsoft illustrate how power in distribution, networks, or technology enables successful expansion.
8. Diversification into unrelated markets is rare and risky, while leveraging existing capabilities for new needs is the most common and effective path to growth.

### Summary:

The podcast explores how successful technology companies grow beyond their original offerings, emphasizing that over half of the top companies' profits now come from second business lines. The core insight is the "seven powers" framework, which helps founders identify sustainable competitive advantages—such as scale economies, network effects, or switching costs—rather than relying solely on product-market fit. The conversation reveals that innovation is not enough; a durable business requires strategic power structures.

Founders must think about transforming their businesses not as a later step, but as an ongoing process, evaluating whether new ventures can build on existing power. Examples like Amazon’s AWS, Nintendo’s shift from playing cards to video games, and Microsoft’s entry into productivity tools demonstrate how existing network or distribution power enables success. The analysis also debunks common myths—such as marketing myopia—by showing that value creation and value capture are separate.

Most successful expansions occur when new products serve different customer needs but leverage shared skills, a concept called "co-action," which accounts for 90% of transformative success in the S&P 100. The takeaway is clear: companies should first deeply understand their current power before expanding, and when launching new ventures, they should focus on repurposing existing capabilities for new jobs to be done. This approach reduces risk and increases the likelihood of sustainable growth.

### FAQs

The Seven Powers framework identifies seven key structures that enable a business to become durable and profitable, like Apple’s iPod. It helps founders move beyond product-market fit by focusing on sustainable competitive advantages, making it easier to evaluate strategic decisions.

Unlike Porter’s Five Forces or disruptive innovation, which focus on industry dynamics or product disruption, Seven Powers emphasizes internal, enduring business structures. It explains long-term profitability through persistent, repeatable competitive advantages rather than short-term market shifts.

Transforming refers to expanding a company into new business areas that leverage existing power structures. It's about identifying whether a new venture can build on current strengths—like network effects or switching costs—rather than starting from scratch.

Business strategy focuses on how a single entity builds sustainable power, while corporate strategy asks whether combining multiple businesses under one roof creates value—such as why Amazon’s AWS is more valuable than just its e-commerce division.

A study of the S&P 100 found that about 50% of their profits come from second or new business lines. This shows that long-term success often depends on evolving and transforming beyond initial product-market fit.

The three most common types are scale economies, network economies, and switching costs. These enable businesses to spread fixed costs, leverage user connections, or create customer loyalty, making them ideal for expansion.

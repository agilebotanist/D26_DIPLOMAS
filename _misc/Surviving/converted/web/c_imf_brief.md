Ms. PEREZ: Hi, everyone. Welcome to the press conference on the October 2026 Global Financial Stability Report. My name is Camila Perez. I'm a communications officer with the IMF. And today I'm joined by Athanasios Vamvakidis. He is the deputy director of the Monetary and Capital Markets Department; Jason Wu, assistant director; and Caio Ferreira, deputy division chief.

We will get started, presenting the key findings of the report, and then we will turn to your questions.

Just to let you know that the report and a blog are now available on IMF.org, so I will invite you to take a look at that.

With that, let me hand it over to you, Thanos, for your opening remarks.

Mr. VAMVAKIDIS: Thank you, Camila. Thank you, everybody, for being here to discuss our Global Financial Stability Report. I will start with some introductory remarks, and then we're looking forward to the discussion with you.

A key question in markets today is whether the pressures we see in the bond market will affect risk sentiment. So far, risk appetite has been very strong. To a large extent, this has to do with very strong earnings, upward growth surprises, but also optimism about AI. This resilience, however, is not necessarily safety. And in our report, we discussed three key risks that could trigger market turmoil, and tightening financial conditions. First, an AI‑related market correction, given very optimistic earnings forecasts and very strong investment plans in the sector in the context of a very heavy reliance on both the real economy and markets on the sector. Second, the evolving risk of higher yields which, as we will discuss today, is driven by multiple forces, depending on the time and the country. And third, the known and very much unknown risk of cyber threats, which could disrupt financial services and weaken confidence. Each of these risks on its own might be manageable. However, it could trigger unresolved and, in some cases, worsening vulnerabilities, with potential systemic implications.

Starting with the banking sector. Under a severe stress test scenario in our report that includes shocks from risk‑off from an AI market correction, shocks in energy prices and in the bond market, including through NBFIs, the banking sector, as a whole, remain resilient. However, there is a tail of banks that will find themselves undercapitalized under stress. And moreover, these are the banks that are the most exposed to NBFIs, the non‑bank financial institutions.

Turning to Non‑Bank Financial Institutions (NBFIs), we focused a lot on this area because we believe this is where risks have migrated since regulation was tightened in the banking sector after the global financial crisis. And we focused on a number of areas. We highlight that for pensions and insurance, we're seeing increasing exposure to illiquid assets that could be a problem under market stress. In private credit, we see increasing leverage in borrowers, which actually could lead through liquidity pressures to restricted credit looking forward in a stress scenario. Hedge funds‑‑and we have an analytical chapter on that‑‑have been expanding rapidly, supporting market conditions but, under stress, could lead to an amplification of shocks through leverage and crowded positioning.

Emerging markets have been resilient. We see actually historically very tight spreads, despite the rate sell‑off. To a large extent, this has to do with strong fundamentals, good policies, but also very strong global risk appetite. However, emerging markets could be affected if the rate sell‑off intensifies. And we're particularly concerned about the depletion of buffers in some low‑rated emerging economies and about increasing reliance to less transparent alternative funding sources in some frontier economies.

Given all this, the policy priority should be to strengthen resilience before new shocks hit the economy to take advantage of the resilience we have seen so far to prepare. Inflation remains high in many economies, and now we're dealing with new inflation pressures. Debt dynamics are worrisome in some cases, and policy room remains much more limited compared to previous periods, in response to previous shocks. It is, therefore, very important for fiscal policy to focus on addressing fiscal imbalances and bringing debt dynamics back to a sustainable path, for monetary authorities to focus on the inflation mandates, communicate clearly, and most importantly, preserve their independence, with their credibility being extremely important to anchor long‑term yields. It is very important also to strengthen cyber resilience and for supervisors to reinforce the monitoring of banks and particularly non‑bank financial institutions to address any systemic risks. The bottom line is that now is the time to strengthen the financial system.

And with these introductory remarks, let's turn the floor back to you. Camila?

Mr. VAMVAKIDIS: I mean, there's no doubt that if you get an AI market correction, it will affect the real economy; it will have broader implications. How severe it is going to be? It depends on the scenario that will unfold.

As the WEO press conference earlier emphasized, AI has been a strong engine for the real economy, one of the key reasons why growth has surprised to the upside. When you look at equity prices, directly or indirectly, stocks related to AI have been the drivers.

It is interesting, actually, that valuations are not stretched. P/E ratios actually have fallen because earnings keep surprising to the upside. So, there is very strong concentration in the AI. We can say it is the single most important engine at this point of the resilience. So, if you get a market correction because earnings disappoint, the very ambitious investment plans don't materialize, or they do and they don't pay off, this will have potentially broader implications.

There is also the risk that the high yields we are seeing could affect earnings; and through this, you are going to get a market correction. It would, as we discuss in our report, cause a tightening of financial conditions. So that is why this is a risk that we emphasize. It's not that we argue that we will see a market correction. But it is a risk, given the heavy reliance on the sector. Another related concern that we emphasize is the circularity of funding in AI, which right now is a positive feedback loop; but if it slows, if it stops either because the funders decide to slow down or if the revenue promises fail to materialize, this is another channel that will turn into a negative feedback loop. So, this heavy reliance on AI is a risk.

Ms. PEREZ: Thank you, Thanos. We're going to go with the gentleman in the first row.

Mr. FERREIRA: I think that the forces that are making cyber threats a global financial stability concern are actually global forces, right? So, there are three major forces. I think one is the increasing digitalization that kind of expands the attack surface. The second one is the increasing concentration and reliance on specific technologies. Think about some specific software or cloud service providers. So, this concentration also makes potential cyber threats more important and potentially more systemic. And finally, the availability of tools to cyber criminals, like‑‑including AI. These are global forces that are impacting all countries and need to be dealt with. So which regions become more vulnerable in this sense are those that do not have appropriate prevention, right? And what we see globally is that prevention has been challenged by a lack of availability of cybersecurity professionals. There are some estimates that consider that it would be necessary \[to have\] maybe 40 percent more people working on cybersecurity than we have right now.

And then on country specifics, I think that, broadly, we see emerging markets as being less prepared by some less resources that they have devoted to that. But, again, cyber threats impact every country. In advanced economies, it's important to improve‑‑to focus on understanding what are the main nodes that can impact the cyber threats and how there are operational connections between different parts of the financial sector. In emerging markets, it's important to further focus on cyber hygiene, increase specific capacity in supervisory agencies, and improve coordination during crises.

Mr. WU: Maybe I can first address the circular financing question.

I think the structure of this financing that we looked at in this GFSR, we looked at 14 public transactions. And what we find is about $650 billion in equity investments, $300 billion in debt investments‑‑Sorry. The other way around. $300 billion in equity, $650 billion in debt, including in off‑balance sheet vehicles, in exchange for $1.4 trillion of future revenue commitments. So perhaps what makes this a little bit unusual compared to previous deals is the future revenue commitment’s part, hence the circularity, in a way.

So the concern here, as Thanos has previously said, is a little bit of a triple whammy. If there are questions about the monetization in AI, the equity investments will deplete in value, the debt may be difficult to service, and the revenue commitments in the future may not materialize. And looking at this, you know, circularity also tells us that there are certain companies and nodes that are particularly exposed or, put differently, if there are, you know, some kind of issues with those entities, then the circularity would pose risks to the market.

Mr. VAMVAKIDIS: To the second part of your question, I mentioned earlier the P/E ratio, price‑to‑earnings ratio, because if we compare it with dot‑com bubble, that's the key difference. We have a situation in which the market has very optimistic earnings forecasts, and then the actual earnings beat these expectations, and the market upgrades the forecast even higher, and earnings again beat the expectations. So, this is what has been going on in recent quarters. And there is a question: Can this continue? It might. But it increasingly becomes more difficult. And increasingly, you have concentration‑‑also addressing the earlier question‑‑that makes you too dependent on this positive feedback loop continuing.

Another key difference is that the exposure of the rest of the world to U.S. assets, particularly AI, is the highest it has ever been. So, this is supporting the global economy through wealth effects today; but if you get a market correction, it can have broader implications.

Ms. PEREZ: Thanks, both. We're going to go with the gentleman in the fifth row, dark jacket, please.

We have seen improvements on the inflation front, which suggests that a perhaps not as tight a monetary policy stance would be appropriate. I think what's important is credibility; in other words, the central bank has to be data‑driven and has to be able to explain the decisions clearly. On strengthening the banking sector, often what we see in emerging economies is, after recapitalization, starting to impose appropriate regulations to prevent risks from building up in the future. Mr. VAMVAKIDIS: If you exclude AI from the stock market, the rest of the stocks so far this year have had a mixed performance. So, you could argue that, at least the first‑round effect of an AI‑market correction would not necessarily affect other stocks. And the truth is that risk sentiment globally is very strong, which is the point with which we started this press conference. So, you could have a widening of spreads; you could have a reduction of risk positions, and again, through this feeding back into the real economy.

Now, as was emphasized, policy room is much more limited compared to past shocks. So, we should not expect macroeconomic policies to come to the rescue. And on fiscal policy, definitely, there's not much room. And on monetary policy, unless you get a deflationary shock, monetary authorities have to focus on their inflation mandate. That is why we discuss these risks in the report.

Now, how negative would such a scenario be? It really depends on the details. But the main concern that we have is that a market correction in AI can have broader implications.

Ms. PEREZ: Thank you. We're going to go with the lady in the fourth row, light or white shirt. Thank you.

QUESTIONER: I just wanted to talk about your warnings about global debt. To what extent is the dynamics in sovereign bonds a disorderly one? Or is it actually just a very normal response to a world where inflation is still persistent and interest rates are rising and we're having a stock market boom? And are you actually seeing falling demand for sovereign debt among investors and creditors?

Mr. VAMVAKIDIS: Now, clearly, the market moves we have seen in rates, some of them have been sharp, to a large extent because of positioning adjustments. Stop losses have been triggered. Having said that, the market has functioned well. There is liquidity. And we believe that it is consistent with fundamentals. Actually, if you look at levels and volatility in the rates market, we are back to the decades before the global financial crisis. So, we're going back to the previous normal. And indeed, the increase in yields started after the pandemic, as the market priced the end of the low inflation era, the end of zero/negative rates and QE. So that was a shift to a new regime.

Having said that, this year we have new pressures that came from the energy shocks that followed the conflict in the Middle East, in a number of cases from already sticky inflation, and a substantial repricing of central banks. So, this pushed yields even higher. In some cases, growth has also surprised to the upside, which contributed, actually shifting the whole yield curve upwards.

Countries with fiscal imbalances have been more vulnerable to these moves. So, although fiscal has not been one of the triggers, it has made countries more vulnerable to these moves, and we're seeing a positive correlation between the change in yields and fiscal imbalances.

The market is concerned about absorption potential of the huge issuance that is coming from the sovereigns and increasingly also from the private sector at a time that central banks are shrinking their balance sheets, and you have to rely more on private investors.

So, putting everything together, you have multiple forces acting at the same time, many of them representing a new regime, risks from terms-of-trade shocks, from energy prices, and depending on the time and the country, some of these forces played more of a role than others. But all this suggests that rates have not overshot fundamentals. There are fundamental drivers that have led to this sell‑off in the rates market. And the silver lining here is that market discipline is back. The market is pricing as it should. Inflation risks, fiscal risks, policy risk, policy uncertainty, and hopefully, as we emphasize, this will contribute to countries putting their houses in order.

It is unusual for yields to be so high and spreads to be so narrow. Mr. WU: Thank you. Yeah, I agree. Now to your question about Morocco. Our World Economic Outlook colleagues have forecasted a projected growth of 4.4 percent for the next year, so this is a fairly solid number, and it reflects agricultural rebound and other nonagricultural activities.

You asked specifically about financial stability. The banking system in Morocco remains sound, liquid, and profitable, and fairly well capitalized, so I think that is important because the banking system serves as the core for the financial system.

That said, I think Morocco is exposed to many of the external forces that we have already discussed, including high energy prices, external pressures, perhaps on currencies, as well. So, building resilience, making sure that fiscal policies are in line and appropriately consolidated would be the way to go.

On the specific recommendation that we have for the use of central counterparties, the idea is that CCPs can actually favor and ensure appropriate market conditions during these times of stress. By reducing counterparty risk, they can ensure that there is more liquidity in the market.

Typically, in emerging markets, we see some limited capacity to deal with these threats, mostly because of an absence of appropriate resources.

# Microsoft Opens Ignite With Copilot Seat Count Still a Mystery and Morgan Stanley Betting on $650

Microsoft opens its Ignite conference on Nov. 17 without having disclosed paid Copilot seat counts in its latest quarterly results, after reporting more than 30 million seats in July. Management instead highlighted usage growth, with daily users up roughly 50% sequentially. Morgan Stanley, led by analyst Adam Wood, published a Nov. 12 note with a $650 base-case target and an $800 bull case, built on Azure's 48% growth, Copilot monetization, the E7 license tier and Foundry's tripling revenue. Microsoft reports fiscal second-quarter results around Jan. 27.

##### Key Elements

Microsoft opens its annual Ignite conference in San Francisco on Nov. 17 with one number conspicuously missing from its recent earnings disclosure: how many people are actually paying for Microsoft 365 Copilot.

The software giant stopped reporting its paid Copilot seat count in its fiscal first-quarter 2027 results released Oct. 28, after three consecutive quarters in which it disclosed the figure. The last update, from July, put the total above 30 million. Chief Executive Satya Nadella is scheduled to deliver the opening keynote on Tuesday morning, joined by Judson Althoff, who runs Microsoft's commercial business.

When an analyst pressed for an update on the October call, Chief Financial Officer Amy Hood was direct. "\[W\]e did not disclose, to answer your direct question, an update on the Copilot number this quarter. We talked about momentum in revenue and usage," she said. Hood noted that Copilot revenue growth accelerated from the prior quarter, with adoption continuing across Microsoft 365, GitHub and security products, but did not provide a dollar figure.

Instead, management pointed to engagement metrics. Nadella said daily Copilot users rose almost 50% from the previous quarter and more than fivefold year over year. Customers holding more than 50,000 Copilot seats increased more than eightfold from a year earlier, and the number of customers using usage-based Copilot credits grew more than 75% in the quarter.

Those figures are strong, but they measure activity rather than committed spending. As more customers shift to consumption-based billing, a seat count may become a less complete gauge of the business over time.

**Why the number matters to the valuation**

At roughly $521, Microsoft trades at about 29 times adjusted earnings, down from about 31 times at its Nov. 4 record close of $555.80. The stock is about 6% below that peak. Investors are paying for years of AI-driven growth, and Copilot is arguably the piece of that story Microsoft now shares the least about.

Microsoft lists the Microsoft 365 Copilot add-on for large businesses at $30 per user per month. Even if all 30 million seats paid full price -- unlikely given discounts and bundling -- the product would generate about $11 billion a year. Against roughly $347 billion in revenue over the last four quarters, that is approximately 3% of the total. Microsoft Cloud alone brought in $63.9 billion in the fiscal first quarter, up 30% year over year.

A paid seat count well above 30 million, or better yet a revenue figure for Copilot, would show the assistant becoming large enough to move results at a company of this scale. Ignite may not deliver either; the January earnings report could be the more likely venue.

**Morgan Stanley's four-part thesis**

Morgan Stanley published a note on Nov. 12 arguing Microsoft belongs meaningfully higher, with a base-case price target of $650 -- about 25% above the $521.13 close on Nov. 11. The firm's bull case reaches $800, while its bear case sits at $300.

The analysis, led by analyst Adam Wood, describes an enterprise AI flywheel built on four layers: Azure compute, Copilot monetization, the E7 premium license tier, and the Foundry platform for custom AI applications.

Azure is the foundation. In fiscal first-quarter 2027, Azure grew 48% year over year in constant currency, six percentage points faster than the prior quarter and above analyst estimates. Amazon Web Services grew 39.3% over the same period. Total revenue came in at $92.3 billion, up 19%, with operating margin near 50%. Capital expenditure doubled to $39 billion as Microsoft builds data center capacity for AI workloads.

The firm also flagged Microsoft's Maia custom chip program as a variable worth watching. Azure currently runs on hardware from Nvidia (NVDA) and other third-party suppliers; progress on Maia economics would add a margin dimension to what is already a growth story.

**The E7 test and the stickiness argument**

Morgan Stanley is watching the E7 enterprise tier closely. E7 bundles Copilot with security and compliance tools at a higher per-seat price than standard Microsoft 365 plans. If large customers migrate toward E7, average revenue per user rises without Microsoft having to add accounts -- a more durable form of growth than new logos.

Management guided second-quarter M365 Commercial Cloud growth to roughly 19% in constant currency. The firm expects Copilot monetization to become more visible in those numbers as the year progresses; the user metrics suggest the base exists, and the open question is conversion.

Foundry, meanwhile, is where enterprises build their own AI applications rather than deploy Copilot as packaged. Revenue from the platform more than tripled year over year in the fiscal first quarter, and the number of customers building multi-model agentic systems on it grew fivefold. More than 19,000 companies now use both Microsoft Fabric and Foundry together, up from more than 17,000 the previous quarter.

Morgan Stanley regards the combined Fabric-plus-Foundry count as one of the cleaner indicators in the dataset, because companies that have organized data in Fabric and run custom models through Foundry face significant cost and disruption in switching infrastructure providers. That stickiness underpins the flywheel concept, and those customers tend to expand Azure spending as workloads grow.

The shift also signals a change in enterprise behavior. Early adoption centered on giving employees access to an assistant; building custom applications requires long-term infrastructure commitments and internal development resources.

**What comes next**

Microsoft's fiscal second-quarter report is projected for Jan. 27, 2027, giving the company roughly ten weeks to produce data that either supports or complicates the Morgan Stanley thesis. Analysts from the firm are scheduled to meet Hood and Microsoft's investor relations team on Nov. 18, conversations that will shape how they size the gap between the current share price and their target.

The stock's 52-week range tells the story of the debate: a low of $349.20 before a recovery above $520 this fall, as the market worked through whether AI capital spending is converting into revenue growth at the scale required to justify it.

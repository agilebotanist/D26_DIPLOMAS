# Solo Founders Now Start Over a Third of New Startups, but the One-Person Unicorn Isn't Here

**URL:** https://startupfortune.com/solo-founders-now-start-over-a-third-of-new-startups-but-the-one-person-unicorn-isnt-here/
**Description:** Solo founders now start 36.3% of new startups, per Carta data. Why the one-person unicorn still has not arrived and what AI did and did not change.
**Scraped:** 2026-11-17T14:55:29.463508+00:00

---

*Every few months someone announces that the first billion-dollar company run by one person is about to appear. It hasn't, as far as anyone can verify. But something real has happened underneath the prediction, and it is changing who starts companies.*

Quick Summary - TLDR:
- Carta's 2025 Solo Founders Report, as cited by the Founder Institute, says startups with a single founder rose from 23.7% of new companies in 2019 to 36.3% in the first half of 2025
- Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei has estimated a 70 to 80% chance that the first one-person billion-dollar company appears as early as 2026, as quoted by Inc.
- The best documented solo exit is Base44, which Wix bought for $80 million in cash in June 2025 after about six months, a big result but far from a billion
- No verified one-person unicorn exists yet, and most of the figures circulating come from promotional blogs
- AI coding tools are the main reason a single person can now ship a product that once needed a small team
- The harder limits are still enterprise sales, trust and the loneliness of doing it all alone

## The number that matters is not a billion

The "solocorn" is a great headline, and it keeps the conversation on a number nobody has hit. The figure worth paying attention to is smaller and already real. Carta's 2025 Solo Founders Report, as cited in a Founder Institute analysis, found that the share of new startups with just one founder climbed from 23.7% in 2019 to 36.3% in the first half of 2025. That measures how many startups begin with one person, not how valuable they become, but it is a large shift in a short time. More than one new company in three now starts without a co-founder.

The obvious reason is the tooling. A founder who can describe a product to an AI coding assistant and get working software back no longer needs to find a technical partner before they start. The part of the job that used to require a second person, building the first version, has become something one person can do over a weekend.

## The prediction everyone quotes

The idea of a one-person billion-dollar company was floated years ago by tech leaders, and it has been repeated so often that it now sounds like a forecast with a date on it. Inc. reported that Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei put the odds at 70 to 80% that the first one-person, billion-dollar company will appear as early as 2026. It is a bold estimate from someone who sells the tools that would make it possible, and it is worth reading with that in mind. Nobody has verified such a company so far.

The best-documented solo success is far smaller. Wix bought Base44, an AI app-building startup largely built by one founder, for $80 million in cash in June 2025, roughly six months after it launched. That is an extraordinary return for half a year of work. It is also a long way from a unicorn, and a single acquisition is a data point, not a trend.

## What AI actually changed, and what it did not

AI has collapsed the cost of the first version. It has not removed the other parts of a company. A solo founder still has to find customers, earn the trust of anyone paying real money, handle support, and make decisions under pressure with nobody to argue with. Enterprise buyers in particular tend to want a team, a security review and someone to call at two in the morning. Those are the places where a one-person company runs out of road.

There is also the human cost that the success stories skip. Doing everything alone is isolating, and several of the people writing about the solo-founder boom point to loneliness as a risk in its own right. A co-founder is not only extra labour. They are someone who tells you that the idea is wrong.

## A caution about the numbers

Many of the figures around this topic come from promotional sources, including training programs and tool vendors with something to sell, so they are worth checking against the underlying data. Claims about specific solo founders running companies worth millions are common and rarely verified. Where this article cites a number, it names where it came from.

## What to take from it

If you are thinking about starting alone, the odds are better than they were five years ago, and the tools are good enough that the first version is no longer the hard part. The hard part has moved to distribution, trust and staying power. Treat the solocorn as the exception it is, build for the customer in front of you, and be honest about which of the jobs a co-founder would have done you are still doing by hand.

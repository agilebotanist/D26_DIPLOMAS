# Startup Headcount Peaked a Year Before ChatGPT. AI Got the Credit

**URL:** https://businessmodelanalyst.com/startup-headcount-shrinking-ai-funding-cycle/
**Description:** Startup headcount peaked in 2021, a year before ChatGPT. Carta and PitchBook data show the funding cycle set team size first, and AI came second.
**Scraped:** 2026-11-17T14:59:22.363593+00:00

---

*Founders are swapping engineers for agents and telling the Wall Street Journal about it. The hiring data says the money left first, and the tools arrived in a room that was already emptying.*

Startups are getting smaller, and AI is getting the credit. The calendar points somewhere else. LinkedIn data cited by the Wall Street Journal puts the peak in average US startup head count in 2021, and Carta’s records show net hiring at venture-backed companies topping out in January 2022, at more than 55,000 people in a single month, two months before the Federal Reserve’s first rate hike and ten months before ChatGPT launched. Net hiring sat near zero through the funding drought of 2023, then rebounded in 2025, the year coding agents went mainstream. Startup team size follows the funding round first. AI decides which seats stay empty inside it.

Butternut AI makes a no-code website builder with around 10,000 paying customers. It had nine employees at its peak. It has four now, after its founders laid off five engineers late last year and handed the work to agents that run through the night.

Advait Paliwal goes further. He is building a consumer app alone, and he describes his entire setup as 20 Claude and Codex subscriptions running in place of employees. He interviewed four engineering candidates over the past year, a 14-year-old in Belgium among them, and hired none, because briefing a person took as much instruction as briefing Claude, and Claude answered faster.

Put those two stories next to each other and AI looks like the reason startups are shrinking. Put the startup hiring curve next to the venture funding curve and you get a different order of events.

## What Happened

The Wall Street Journal’s [Katherine Bindley reported on September 21](https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/startup-hiring-ai-staffing-8c626f75) that a subset of early-stage founders now asks whether AI can do a job before they hire for it. Some have laid people off. Others have cut contractors and agencies.

The examples are specific. Lindy, which sells an AI teammate that plugs into Slack, has about two dozen employees and has raised $50 million. It laid off a handful of marketers in January during a product pivot and never refilled the roles. Two marketers plus AI agents now cover a user base that has passed half a million. Lindy used to pay five creative agencies for content and video. It pays none today. A marketing-data dashboard that once needed a dedicated builder is now a prompt, according to its growth marketing lead, Haneen Azhar.

AgentCollect, which uses AI agents to chase unpaid invoices, is down to about 30 employees from a peak of 50. Billing coordinators, call-quality evaluators and a product manager are among the roles it has largely automated. CEO John Banner frames it as a hiring philosophy about judgment and creativity rather than a cost program.

Y Combinator supplied two lean success stories from its 2024 batches: Emergent at $15 million in annualized revenue with 15 people, and Retell AI at $60 million with about 40.

The statistic doing the heavy lifting comes from LinkedIn. Startups founded in 2022 with at least five employees in their first year averaged 20.7 employees by year three. The equivalent cohort founded in 2018 averaged 32.5. That is a 36% smaller company at the same age.

The investors quoted in the piece added the caveats. Liz Wessel of First Round Capital said staying small depends on the product and how fast revenue grows, and that hyperscaling companies still fight for engineers. Aileen Lee of Cowboy Ventures said midmarket and enterprise buyers paying $60,000 to $75,000 a year want to buy from a company with humans in it. She also said the size of the check sets how long a startup can go without hiring, and that a Series A now expects $4 million to $5 million in first-year revenue.

That last pair of points is the story the headline skipped.

## The Backstory

Start with the LinkedIn cohorts and check what year it was when each one turned three.

The 2018 class hit its third year around 2020 and 2021. US venture investment in 2021 reached $358.2 billion across 19,634 deals, according to the [PitchBook-NVCA Venture Monitor](https://pitchbook.com/news/reports/q4-2025-pitchbook-nvca-venture-monitor), the richest year the industry had ever recorded. The 2022 class was born in the year the Federal Reserve began raising rates, in March. Deal value fell to $236.2 billion that year and to $168.8 billion in 2023, a 53% drop from the peak, while deal count fell 22% to 15,379.

So the comparison behind the headline sets a cohort that grew up in the most generous funding market on record against a cohort that grew up in the harshest one in a decade. Any tool that launched in between will look like the cause.

Carta, which runs cap tables for more than 45,000 US startups, shows the same break from the payroll side. From 2019 through 2022, startups on its platform added 865,000 net employees. From the start of 2023 through the spring of 2025, they added 31,000. In January 2022 alone, net headcount grew by more than 55,000, with new hires outnumbering departures 3.8 to 1. Layoffs peaked a year later, in January 2023.

[Peter Walker, who runs Carta’s insights team, laid out the sequence](https://carta.com/data/linkedin-startup-headcount-frozen-2025/) in May 2025. Rates moved in 2022, funding dried up and layoffs followed. The years 2023 and 2024 were about belt tightening, and ARR per employee became the metric everyone wanted to show. He only suspected AI from mid-2024 onward.

Carta’s compensation team read the same chart a year later and tied the break to ChatGPT’s launch in November 2022. Both readings fit the start date, because the two shocks landed within weeks of each other. ChatGPT went public on November 30, 2022. On December 14, the Fed lifted its target range to 4.25% to 4.50%. The starting line cannot tell you which one did it.

The finish line can.

## The Plan

Every founder in the Journal’s piece is running the same plan: stay small for as long as possible, and ask the software first. If that plan were the main force setting startup team size, you would expect the thinnest hiring year to be the one where the software got best.

That year was 2025. Anthropic’s Claude Code and OpenAI’s Codex went from previews to tools engineers used all day, and agents started working the night shift Butternut describes. Carta’s data shows 2025 as the recovery year for startup hiring, with monthly net headcount growth reaching its highest levels since 2022. January 2026 added 5,652 net employees, in line with most of 2025.

The money came back in the same year. US venture deal value climbed to $339.4 billion in 2025 by PitchBook’s first tally, within reach of the 2021 record.

The rebound is partial, and the reason it stalls at roughly a tenth of the 2022 peak is also about capital. The venture recovery landed at the top of the market.

US startups raised $412.7 billion in the first half of 2026, more than in any previous full year, and AI companies took 86% of it. Rounds under $100 million drew $51.4 billion, or 12.5% of the total, [according to the Q2 2026 Venture Monitor](https://nvca.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Q2-2026-PitchBook-NVCA-Venture-Monitor.pdf). Their share was 43.8% in 2024 and 33.1% in 2025.

Run those shares against the annual totals and the pool that funds companies the size of Butternut or Lindy looks flat. It works out to roughly $93 billion in 2024, $105 billion to $112 billion in 2025 depending on which PitchBook revision you use, and about $103 billion annualized in 2026, on our math. The headline number is on pace for almost four times its 2024 level. The money reaching a four-person startup stayed near $100 billion, and 2025 spread it across 9.6% more deals.

That is what Aileen Lee’s $4 million to $5 million Series A bar looks like from the founder’s chair. Investors with a flat pool and more companies to choose from ask for more revenue before they write the check, and a founder who cannot raise until she hits that number does not hire until she hits it either.

The cleanest counterexample sits inside the AI-native world. Lovable spent early 2026 advertising one of the best revenue-per-employee figures in software. Then it raised $400 million in August and [announced plans to grow headcount 50% to 450](https://businessmodelanalyst.com/lovable-series-c-cost-of-goods-capital/). The tools in August were the tools in July. The bank balance was $400 million larger.

## The Business Model Angle

**Headcount is a runway decision before it is a technology decision.** Payroll is the largest fixed cost most startups carry, so team size falls out of a simple division: cash in the bank over monthly burn, set against the number of months until the next round’s milestone. When that milestone is $4 million to $5 million in revenue, founders size the team to reach it without running out of money. AI changes which roles fill the budget. The round sets the budget.

**Small is now a fundraising asset, and the metric came before the tools.** ARR per employee became the number investors wanted during the 2023 and 2024 belt tightening, before coding agents shipped. Founders telling reporters they went from nine people to four are pitching capital efficiency to the people who reward it. AI gave an existing scorecard a better story, the same way the Great Flattening at large companies turned a cost decision into [an org-design announcement measured in the wrong unit](https://businessmodelanalyst.com/uber-micro-teams-manager-cuts-span-of-control/).

**The replacement is rented at a price someone else sets.** Paliwal’s 20 subscriptions cost somewhere between $400 and $4,000 a month at list prices of $20 to $200 per seat, or $4,800 to $48,000 a year. The median US software developer earned about $133,000 in May 2024, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, so the math favors the subscriptions by a wide margin. The contract terms do not. A salary is a price the founder negotiates once a year. A consumer plan is a price the vendor can change on a Tuesday, and Anthropic stopped letting its subscriptions power third-party agent tools in April 2026, pushing those users onto metered billing, [as Madrona documented](https://www.madrona.com/price-of-tokenmaxxing-claude-explosive-growth-cost-of-intelligence/). The top Claude plan also sells its usage as a multiple of a number the company never publishes, [a pricing gap we traced through the stolen-session market](https://businessmodelanalyst.com/claude-stolen-session-flat-rate-arbitrage/). A company whose engineering team is 20 consumer seats runs its biggest cost line with no unit and no contract.

**Humans come back where the buyer can see them.** Lee’s point about $60,000 enterprise contracts shows up in Carta’s hiring mix: sales roles made up nearly 20% of new startup hires in 2024, up from 14.8% in 2020. The seats AI empties first sit behind the product. The seats that return sit in front of the customer, in sales, onboarding and account management, where a buyer checks whether the vendor will still exist next year.

## The Risk

The case studies describe real substitutions. Capital does not explain Lindy dropping five agencies to zero, or AgentCollect choosing not to backfill billing coordinators while the business grows. Those are decisions about work, made by people who had the option to hire.

The start date is a tie, and we are not claiming otherwise. ChatGPT and 4%-plus interest rates arrived in the same weeks, and Carta’s own analysts split on which one broke the hiring curve. The argument here is narrower: the timing cannot prove AI did it, and the 2025 rebound fits the funding story better than the tools story.

The rebound may also hide composition. Carta says hardware led 2025 hiring with 1.7 hires per departure, ahead of medical devices, healthtech and SaaS at 1.4. If the physical economy carried the recovery while software startups kept shrinking, the aggregate flatters the capital thesis.

The strongest evidence for the AI reading sits in the per-stage numbers. Walker reported that the average Series A startup raising in 2025 had 14 full-time employees, down from 22 in 2022, and Carta puts the median seed team at four. Those companies closed the same round with fewer people in a year when funding recovered. That is what you would expect if AI lets a smaller team hit the same milestone, and it is the number to watch.

Some of the headline figures are soft. Y Combinator’s examples are annualized run rates, not audited revenue, and founders who talk to the Journal about staying lean are a self-selected group. Census data also cuts against the grand version of the story: the count of one-person businesses clearing $1 million grew just 0.22% in 2023, as we found when we looked at [the rise of the one-person company](https://businessmodelanalyst.com/the-rise-of-the-one-person-company/). If AI were already reorganizing small firms at scale, you would expect that number to move first.

## Quick Questions

**Are startups smaller because of AI?** Partly. Startup hiring collapsed in 2022 and 2023 as venture funding fell 53% from its 2021 peak, before AI coding agents existed. AI now influences which roles founders leave unfilled inside budgets the funding market sets.

**How small are startups today?** Carta puts the median seed-stage team at four employees. The average Series A company raising in 2025 had 14 full-time staff, down from 22 in 2022.

**Did startup hiring recover?** Partly. Net hiring on Carta rebounded in 2025 to its highest level since 2022, and January 2026 added 5,652 net employees. That is still about 90% below January 2022.

**Why is venture money at a record while small startups stay small?** The recovery went to the top. Rounds of $100 million or more took 87.5% of the $412.7 billion raised in the first half of 2026. Deals below that size drew $51.4 billion.

**What does an AI-only engineering setup cost?** Twenty consumer AI subscriptions cost $400 to $4,000 a month at list prices, far below one engineer’s salary. The vendor controls the price and the usage limits, and can change both.

## The Business Model Analyst Take

The Journal’s founders are telling the truth about their own companies. Butternut did replace five engineers with agents, and Lindy did fire its agencies. The mistake is reading a few dozen founder decisions as the cause of a curve that bent in 2022, when the check shrank.

Startup team size tracks what a round can pay for until the next round. AI moved the line on which jobs are worth paying for, and it handed founders a better answer when an investor asks why the team is so small. The next time venture money reaches rounds under $100 million again, watch the Series A headcount number. If it climbs with the money, the funding cycle set the size. If it stays at 14 while the checks grow, the agents did.

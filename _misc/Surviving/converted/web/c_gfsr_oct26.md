#### Beneath resilient financial markets, risks remain elevated and vulnerabilities persist

Financial markets have remained resilient, supported by solid economic growth and strong corporate earnings, but financial stability risks remain elevated. Three salient risks could trigger a sharp tightening of financial conditions and market turmoil. Weaker or less durable investment and profitability in artificial intelligence could lead to a correction in AI-related asset prices, amplified by rising leverage and concentrated exposures. Resurgent inflation or fiscal concerns could drive a sharp rise in sovereign bond yields, with potentially broad repercussions across financial markets. Meanwhile, increasingly sophisticated cyber threats pose growing risks as finance becomes more digital and interconnected.

Underlying financial vulnerabilities could amplify these risks and turn market stress into systemic events. Although the global banking system remains broadly resilient, pockets of weaker banks persist. Liquidity mismatches among insurers and pension funds have increased, while rising leverage and contingent liquidity risks in private credit could amplify stress. While hedge funds support market functioning in calm times, their use of leverage and exposure to crowded trades could exacerbate market stress too and create spillovers to prime brokers. Some emerging markets have seen policy buffers erode, and frontier economies have increasingly relied on less transparent forms of financing. Finally, although markets for tokenized assets remain small and could offer efficiency gains in several areas, they can create new channels for the buildup of leverage and mismatches in liquidity and maturity, in addition to elevating risks related to technology and infrastructure.

These vulnerabilities underscore the need for policies that strengthen resilience, enhance transparency, contain excessive leverage, and improve preparedness for future shocks.

###### Chapter 2: Hedge Funds and Financial Stability

Hedge funds have become increasingly important participants in global financial markets. Although the sector remains relatively small within nonbank finance, its footprint has grown rapidly. Globally, hedge funds now hold about $13 trillion in assets, more than three times their 2013 level. Unlike most other financial intermediaries, hedge funds also face relatively few constraints on their investment strategies and borrowing, allowing them to adjust their exposures across markets rapidly. The chapter examines the evolution of the hedge fund sector and its implications for financial stability. The findings show that hedge funds can support market liquidity, price discovery, and risk sharing in calm times. During stress, however, leverage, crowded positions, and correlated investor redemptions can trigger synchronized deleveraging and asset sales, increasing volatility and straining market liquidity. Moreover, stress can spill over to prime brokers and magnify global risk shocks in markets with larger hedge fund footprints, particularly where liquidity is thin. Strengthening resilience requires better data for systemwide risk assessments, prudential measures calibrated to the nature and source of hedge fund vulnerabilities, complemented by credible central bank backstops to address severe dysfunctions in core markets.

###### Chapter 3: Scaling Tokenization: New Efficiencies, New Vulnerabilities

Tokenization, the representation of assets and liabilities on programmable distributed ledgers, has the potential to transform financial markets. By integrating functions traditionally performed by multiple intermediaries onto a shared platform, tokenization can streamline the asset life cycle, reducing frictions, improving transparency, and enabling greater automation.

Yet tokenized markets remain small and fragmented. Their future development will depend, in part, on progress in addressing four interrelated constraints: legal certainty, regulatory clarity, interoperability, and the availability of settlement assets.

Evidence from tokenized equity markets points to new functionalities, including 24/7 trading, fractional ownership, and emerging on-chain price discovery. However, largely because of their limited scale, these markets remain less liquid, more volatile, and more fragmented than their traditional counterparts. As tokenization grows, it could improve market efficiency while also introducing new risks. In particular, it may heighten technology and infrastructure dependencies and create new channels through which traditional vulnerabilities, such as liquidity risk, interconnectedness, and leverage, can propagate and amplify shocks.

To support safe and efficient market development, policymakers should adopt a technology-neutral approach that removes unnecessary barriers to tokenization while safeguarding financial stability.

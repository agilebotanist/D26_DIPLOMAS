## Report on the PhD manuscrit of Ildar Nigmatullin called « ReQuirements as CODE :

## une approche orientée objet pour les exigences de sécurité »

The domain of the thesis is that of security requirements, whose objective is to ensure that a system satisfies security constraints. These requirements are often expressed in natural language, which raises several problems, the main one being the difficulty of ensuring traceability between textual requirements and their implementation. Ildar Nigmatullin proposes a solution to these problems through a code-oriented approach to specifying, organizing, reusing, and verifying requirements through execution. His approach is generic and applies to requirements in various contexts, although the thesis is presented as a more specific contribution to security requirements, notably through an industrial case study conducted during a European research project. The proposed approach is scientifically relevant, and its usability is clearly demonstrated by this case study.

The manuscript, written in English, is of very high quality. It is well structured, easy to read, and maintains scientific rigor throughout. Ildar Nigmatullin has taken care to clearly position, from a scientific perspective, the context of the thesis, the scientific problem, the objectives and research questions, and then his contributions, comparing them with existing approaches in the literature.

Chapter 1 is the introduction to the thesis. The research problem is clearly presented: how to fill the gap between security requirements documented in natural language (with the resulting ambiguities and lack of precision) and their implementation, verification, and reuse in the software system, as well as their maintenance when, for example, security standards change and requirements need to be adapted. The proposal of Ildar Nigmatullin is to define these requirements as executable object-oriented code artifacts that are integrated into the software lifecycle. The thesis also aims to investigate, in practical terms, the benefits and usability of this method for managing and verifying security requirements for engineers.

Chapter 2 is an initial state-of-the-art review that establishes the general context of the thesis by explaining what security requirements are, their importance in software development, how they are formalized, and how they can be reused. The chapter concludes by reviewing the principles of verification and validation in software engineering.

1/4

Chapter 3 is a more specific state-of-the-art review focusing on requirements engineering methods, particularly those that enable the transition from the specification of requirements to their implementation and verification. Following a rigorous selection of papers, 13 approaches or categories of approaches are studied. The contexts are very diverse, ranging from approaches primarily oriented toward modeling to code-oriented or test-oriented approaches, some of which were not originally designed specifically to manage requirements. Formal methods are deliberately excluded because of the difficulty engineers may encounter in learning to use them. Based on the needs identified in the introduction and two existing classifications, seven criteria to be satisfied in requirements management are defined: maintenance (including traceability), validation and verification, reusability, seamlessness integration (from specification to implementation), the definition of non-functional requirements, ease of use for a software engineer, and the availability of tool support. The 13 approaches are then reexamined according to these seven criteria. The conclusion is that none of them satisfies all seven criteria simultaneously. The objective of the thesis is therefore to propose an approach that satisfies these seven criteria as fully as possible. This chapter could have benefited from greater clarity if each of the 13 approaches had been presented together with its evaluation against the seven criteria (there is a gap of at least 15 pages between the description of an approach and the section evaluating it against the criteria).

Chapter 4 presents the main contribution of the thesis, the RQCODE approach (ReQuirements as CODE), which formalizes requirements in the form of executable Java classes defining an operation for verifying a requirement. Java was chosen because it is one of the most widely used programming languages, which should facilitate its adoption by software engineers. The use of an object-oriented language, through inheritance between classes, makes it possible to organize, structure hierarchically, and specialize requirements in relation to one another, as well as to reuse them in other projects. The approach proposes a process for moving from the textual definition of requirements to a hierarchy of Java classes in the expected format. It consists first of breaking down and logically organizing the requirements, and then mapping each requirement to a Java class. Traceability between the requirement text and the code that implements it is ensured through naming constraints and the inclusion in the code of textual attributes containing details from the textual definition. The approach is illustrated using the example of an automated teller machine. This example is typically something that could have been implemented using existing testing tools (JUnit in Java, for example). It would therefore have been interesting to have a comparison with a test-based implementation.

Thanks to these characteristics, which make it possible to specify, implement, and then verify a requirement through the same Java class, the criteria defined in the previous chapter are globally satisfied, making the approach scientifically and technically relevant. Nevertheless, I see a few limitations. In the process, the initial step of manually analyzing the requirements is important and complex, but this step is relatively underdeveloped. It is unfortunate, for example, that the manuscript does not provide more details on how the criteria in Table 4.1 are applied. A complete example based on a specific case would have been interesting. Another limitation of this process is that it is entirely manual: there is no tooling, or even, for example, configuration files that would make it possible to formalize more precisely the link between a textual requirement and its Java class. As it stands, this traceability relies on good practice rules followed by the software engineer. This could cause problems for future maintenance of the requirements if these rules are not properly followed in a project.

Chapter 5 presents the second contribution of the thesis, namely the study of the practical usability of the approach in the field of security. To this end, it begins with an industrial

case study conducted in the context of a European research project. This case study is interesting for three reasons. First, it concerns a real case of system security under Windows, involving 256 requirements defined in a standardized Microsoft document. In total, 165 requirements were implemented using the approach, demonstrating its usability (the remaining cases cannot be implemented through code and must be verified manually or using other tools). Second, the software system studied does not consist of Java code to be extended with requirement verification, which demonstrates the ability of the approach to be applied in various contexts. Third, an extended version of RQCODE makes it possible to add corrective actions that are automatically triggered when a requirement is not satisfied, demonstrating that the approach can go beyond simply verifying requirements. A questionnaire completed by the industrial partner led to the conclusion that, overall, the engineers found the approach relevant and more effective than what they had been doing until then. The limitation, mentioned in the manuscript, is that this case study only includes feedback from a single industrial partner. This study also demonstrated that managing different versions of the same requirement could be done simply by maintaining the Java classes on a Git server, which facilitates maintenance and evolution.

The second part of Chapter 5 is a study conducted with software engineering students. They were asked to implement a security requirement using RQCODE and then answer a questionnaire. Here again, the responses indicate that, overall, the approach is relevant and effective for implementing and verifying requirements. The important limitation, mentioned in the manuscript, is that these are students who do not have the experience of software engineers working in industry and who have less knowledge on the field of requirements engineering. Nevertheless, the results of these two questionnaires are positive regarding the value, benefits, and usability of the approach.

Chapter 6 provides a critical discussion of the RQCODE approach. Throughout the chapter, the benefits and limitations of the approach are presented objectively. First, the research questions from Chapter 1 are examined in relation to the responses to the two surveys. The results indicate that all the required aspects are generally addressed by the approach (the ability to formalize a security requirement, usability, reusability, support for verification, traceability, and maintainability). Next, the seven criteria from Chapter 3 are evaluated for the approach, and it is compared with the 13 approaches selected in that state-of-the-art chapter. The conclusion is that the criteria relating to maintenance, validation and verification, reusability, seamlessness integration (from specification to implementation), ease of use for a software engineer, and the availability of tooling are fully satisfied. Only the criterion concerning the definition of non-functional requirements is partially satisfied. Personally, I am somewhat more critical regarding the traceability and seamlessness integration criteria, as mentioned previously. On these points, it would have been possible to go further. Similarly, the criterion concerning available tooling relies heavily on what the existing ecosystem around Java or DevOps pipelines could make possible, but without this having been investigated further in the thesis (this is, in fact, one of the future research directions identified in the thesis), with the exception of the use of Git. Finally, the last section evaluates the ability of the approach to define security requirements according to security categories (access control, integrity, confidentiality, etc.): all categories are covered, some fully and others partially. Overall, this chapter is interesting and important, as it puts the approach into perspective in relation to the objectives of the thesis and the state of the art.

Chapter 7 concludes the manuscript by reviewing the characteristics and benefits of the RQCODE approach, before presenting perspectives for improving it and integrating it into tools.

3/4

The results of the thesis have been published at an international conference, at a workshop of an international conference, and as a contribution to a book chapter.

In conclusion, with a manuscript of very high quality, Ildar Nigmatullin proposes an interesting and innovative code-oriented approach to managing security requirements. This approach has been validated through an industrial case study. For all these reasons, I am fully in favor of Ildar Nigmatullin being authorized to defend his thesis in order to obtain the doctoral degree in Computer Science from the University of Toulouse.

# Bachelor thesis, stage "Chapters 1 and 2": grading rubric for supervisors and TAs

Written for: supervisors and teaching assistants of the thesis group.

## Scope of this stage

The stage covers the draft of chapter 1 (introduction) and chapter 2 (literature review). Experiments do not exist yet, so the plan is judged, not results. The abstract and chapters 3 and later are final-stage items and are not assessed, even if they are placeholders. What is present is checked for template conformity (title page, contents, numbering).

The rubric follows the Innopolis Thesis Guidelines: section 3.4 for the introduction (problem, importance, what is known and unknown, hypotheses or solutions, research questions, structure outline), section 3.5 for the literature review (relevant prior work, classification and evaluation of methods and metrics, justification of the chosen method) and section 4.3 for the assessment criteria.

## Written work, 100 points

| Part | Points | What is assessed |
|---|---|---|
| A. Problem, goal, research questions | 25 | The problem is stated concretely and motivated by evidence, not by assertion. Goal, tasks and research questions (or hypotheses) are consistent with each other and testable. The scope is realistic for a bachelor thesis. The gap is visible already in the introduction. |
| B. Literature review | 35 | Breadth and relevance of sources, and their quality (share of peer-reviewed and primary sources against grey literature). About 20 sources at this stage, about 40 at the defence. Critical synthesis that compares works instead of listing them. An explicit answer to "who solved something similar before" and an explicit research gap. Classification of methods and metrics, and a justified choice of method. |
| C. Method and plan | 25 | Soundness and feasibility of the approach. Metrics that are numbers. Dataset and ground truth, with a named evaluator. Baselines. Number of runs and a statistical plan. Threats to validity. A definition of done. Chapters 1 and 2 may only sketch the plan, but a sketch that can be checked scores higher than a promise. |
| D. Structure and writing | 15 | Logical structure and a consistent chapter map. Clear academic language in the author's own words. Citations in the text match the reference list, and entries are complete. Figures and tables where they help. Repetition and padding are penalised. |

Bands for each part, as a share of the part's points: 90 to 100 percent is excellent and near-final for this stage, 75 to 89 is good with minor gaps, 60 to 74 is acceptable with clear gaps, below 60 needs major rework.

## Oral component

Where students presented at the colloquium, an oral component of 15 percent is added. It is the mean of the colloquium marks for theme, literature and experiment (each 1 to 5), multiplied by 20. Where there is no oral mark, the consultant's grade can stand in for it.

Raw grade = 0.85 x written score + 0.15 x oral component.

## Calibration and letters

The written scale is anchored to a final text, where 90 and above means a near-final draft. A two-chapter draft cannot reach that, so raw grades sit low for this stage. We recommend adding a flat 9 points to the raw grade, capped at 100, so that most drafts land in A, B or C and the band below C is kept for very poor work. The shift keeps the ranking and the gaps between students.

Letter thresholds: A is 90 to 100, B is 80 to 89.99, C is 70 to 79.99. A grade below 70 is reported as below C.

## How the evidence is collected

Read each draft in full. Count the references and check that every citation in the text resolves to an entry in the list. Spot-check at least three references that carry key claims against the source: the paper exists, the authors and year are right, and it says what the text says. Metadata errors in generated or copied bibliographies are common, so a wrong author list is a finding to report, not a reason to assume the paper is invented.

Record two or three concrete strengths and four to six concrete weaknesses for each thesis. Every weakness names a location in the text and states what to change. Feedback to students is written neutrally, as a record of what was discussed and what is to be done, without addressing the student directly.

## Typical profiles at this stage

An A draft states a falsifiable gap against named prior work, compares sources in a table or a structured argument, and gives a concrete plan with numbers, a dataset, a baseline and threats to validity.

A B draft has a clear problem and a real synthesis, with a plan that still lacks several numbers or a decision on the data.

A C draft states the topic and explains the background correctly, but the review mostly restates standards or sources without a gap, or the plan is a general intention.

A draft below C has no usable plan, or leaves out a part of the problem that the approach depends on, so the chosen direction cannot be evaluated.

This is a summary of what was seen across the submitted drafts of chapter 1 (introduction) and chapter 2 (literature review). It contains no individual assessments. Each student receives personal feedback separately. The abstract and chapters 3 and later are not assessed at this stage.

What worked well

  • Most drafts state the problem concretely and link it to prior work, and several turn a reviewed study into a falsifiable gap.
  • The strongest literature chapters do not list papers one after another. They group the sources, say what each group shows and where it stops, and end with a table or paragraph that names the gap the thesis addresses.
  • Definitions that are stated operationally, for example a term broken into conditions that can be checked, made the later plan much easier to evaluate.
  • Several students also separate their own interpretation from what a source reports, which is good practice.

What most drafts still need

TopicWhat was seen and what to do
Numbers in the plan Almost every plan lacks numbers. A plan is checkable when it states the number of runs or cases, the statistical test, the thresholds used, and a definition of done. At this stage the plan may be a sketch, but it should name the dataset or the data source, the baseline, the metric and the way the result will be judged.
Ground truth Ground truth is the most frequent open question. If a result is compared with a reference, the text should say who or what provides the reference, for example a labelled dataset, an expert, or an automatic judge verified by an expert.
Threats to validity Threats to validity are missing or limited to one paragraph. A short subsection covering the main ones (synthetic against real data, circularity between the benchmark and the method, non-determinism of the tools, contamination of data, small samples) improves the chapter considerably.
Scope The scope is often too wide. Several drafts carry four or five research questions, or three separate lines of work. A finished study of one or two questions is better than an unfinished study of four, and the extra lines can move to future work.
Motivation The motivation sometimes rests on assertion. A concrete example, a number from a source, or a documented incident is stronger than a general statement that a problem is important. Hypotheses or testable research questions should follow from the gap, as the Thesis Guidelines ask for in the introduction.
Literature review In the literature review, the review should answer the question "who solved something similar before, on what data, with what result". Narrow sub-areas that the thesis depends on need to be covered, and textbook material that does not serve the argument can be shortened. If the review states that no earlier work exists, describe how the search was done.
References References need a check. Verify every entry against the publisher page, DBLP or Google Scholar: authors, title, venue, year and pages. Several lists had wrong author names or venue labels even though the papers exist. Prefer the published version over the preprint when both exist, and keep grey sources (documentation, model cards, blog posts) to cases where nothing else is available. Page or section pointers for key claims are good practice.
Writing Writing can be tightened. Repeated statements of the same gap, long runs of hedging ("may", "does not necessarily") and recurring sentence patterns make a text longer and weaker. Aim for the author's own words and concrete claims. Long dashes in English prose should be recast with commas, colons or new sentences.
Figures and tables Figures and tables are almost absent. A pipeline or testbed diagram and a comparison table of related work would help most drafts.

Before the next draft

  1. Check that the title, the goal, the tasks and the research questions say the same thing.
  2. Add the missing numbers to the plan.
  3. Name the ground truth.
  4. Add a short threats-to-validity subsection.
  5. Verify the reference list.
  6. Add one figure and one comparison table.
